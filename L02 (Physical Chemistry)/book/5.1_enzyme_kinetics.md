# Enzyme kinetics

We now turn to a topic that serves as a perfect application of the law of mass action: _enzyme kinetics_. Enzymes are proteins that act as _catalysts_ for biochemical reactions, meaning they speed up reactions without being consumed in the process.

There are many different physical and chemical mechanisms for how an enzyme can speed up a process. We won't go into the fine molecular details, but from a conceptual perspective, we can simplify by saying that enzymes lower the activation energy barrier of a chemical reaction. This can, for example, be achieved by the enzyme binding to an intermediate state in the reaction, stabilizing it.

Recall Arrhenius' law, which stated that the rate constant of a reaction can be written as:

```{math}
:label: eq:arrhenius_enzymes
k = Ae^{-E_{\rm a}/RT}
```

Since the reaction rate is proportional to the _exponential_ of the activation energy ($E_{\rm a}$), even a relatively small reduction in this barrier can cause a massive increase in reaction speed. Because of this, enzymes are usually highly efficient and can speed up reactions by many orders of magnitude (by a factor of $10^7$ or more in some cases)!

The drastic increase in reaction speed caused by enzymes is hugely important for cellular function. For many spontaneous reactions in the cell, their actual reaction rates are so slow that the molecules are effectively stable. But as soon as a bit of the appropriate enzyme is added, things start happening rapidly. In this sense, enzymes can be thought of as "on-buttons" for various biological processes that are otherwise lying in standby.

An excellent example of this is the energy carrier molecule ATP. ATP carries a fair bit of chemical energy and will spontaneously dissociate to release it. Luckily, this spontaneous dissociation happens so slowly that ATP is stable enough to be an effective energy carrier. The cell can produce ATP through metabolism and let it float around the cytoplasm for a while without fear of wasting energy. Distributed throughout the cell are molecular machines equipped with enzymes that speed up the dissociation of ATP, harnessing the released energy in the process. Such enzymatic machines are known as _ATPases_, and we will see several examples of them later in the course.

## Enzymes change reaction speed, not direction

It is important to note that an enzyme can never alter the net change in Gibbs free energy ($\Delta G$) of a reaction, and will therefore never be able to change the _direction_ of a reaction. An enzyme cannot trigger a reaction that is not spontaneous on its own; it can only speed it up. The upside, however, is that the enzyme molecules do not get used up in the reaction, and therefore even a tiny amount of enzyme is usually enough to trigger a massive, continuous process.
