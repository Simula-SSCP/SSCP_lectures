# The sodium-calcium exchanger (NCX)

Unlike SERCA, the NCX is an _exchanger_, not an ATPase. It does not burn ATP for energy. Instead, it pushes one calcium ion out of the cell against its gradient by simultaneously allowing three sodium ions to flow _into_ the cell down their massive concentration gradient.

## The reversal potential

Because NCX couples two passive transports, it is governed by the net change in Gibbs free energy. The exchanger will stop pumping when the energy pushing sodium in exactly balances the energy pushing calcium out. This yields the **reversal potential** of the NCX:

$$
E_{\mathrm{NCX}} = 3E_{\mathrm{Na}} - 2E_{\mathrm{Ca}}
$$

If the membrane voltage $V$ is below $E_{\mathrm{NCX}}$, the exchanger operates in "forward mode" (pumping Ca$^{2+}$ out). If $V$ rises above $E_{\mathrm{NCX}}$, it operates in "reverse mode" (pumping Ca$^{2+}$ in).

## The Luo-Rudy formulation

Because NCX transports a net charge ($3\mathrm{Na}^+$ in vs. $1\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$ out), it generates an electrical current! This makes modeling it much more complex than SERCA, because its rate depends heavily on the membrane voltage.

A highly realistic, widely used formulation was developed by Luo & Rudy (1994):

$$
I_{\mathrm{NaCa}} = k_{\mathrm{NaCa}} \frac{[\mathrm{Na}^+]^3_{\mathrm{i}} [\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{e}} \exp\left[\eta V \frac{F}{RT}\right] - [\mathrm{Na}^+]^3_{\mathrm{e}} [\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{i}} \exp\left[(\eta-1) V \frac{F}{RT}\right]}{\left(K_{\mathrm{m, Na}}^3 + [\mathrm{Na}^+]_{\mathrm{e}}^3\right) \left(K_{\mathrm{m, Ca}} + [\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{e}}\right) \left(1 + k_{\mathrm{sat}} \exp\left[(\eta-1) V \frac{F}{RT}\right]\right)}
$$

While this equation is conceptually heavy and computationally expensive, it brilliantly captures the complex dependencies on voltage, sodium, and calcium concentrations without requiring any new differential states.

## NCX and afterdepolarizations

The fact that NCX is electrogenic makes it a prime suspect in **arrhythmogenesis**.

Imagine a diseased cell at rest. If the SR suddenly misfires and releases a spontaneous wave of calcium into the cytosol, the NCX will immediately activate to clear it. However, because it brings 3 $\mathrm{Na}^+$ ions in for every 1 $\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$ it pumps out, it generates a net _inward_ (depolarizing) current!

If this NCX current is strong enough, it can depolarize the resting membrane all the way to the sodium threshold, spontaneously firing a new, lethal action potential known as a **Delayed Afterdepolarization (DAD)**.
