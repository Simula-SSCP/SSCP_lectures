# The SERCA pump

The SERCA pump (Sarco/Endoplasmic Reticulum Calcium-ATPase) uses the energy stored in ATP to pump calcium from the cytosol back into the SR.

Because the SR membrane is packed with these pumps, SERCA is a **high-capacity** pump. Because it can maintain high activity even when cytosolic calcium drops very low, it is also a **high-affinity** pump. Let us look at how researchers model this pump, moving from simple approximations to complex biophysical reality.

## 1. The unidirectional pump model

The most basic feature of the SERCA pump is that it binds two calcium ions, transports them into the SR, and releases them. Because it transports two ions at a time, we can model it as an enzymatic process using a Michaelis-Menten (Hill) equation with a Hill coefficient of 2.

If we assume the pump only runs forward (ignoring reverse leaks), we get the unidirectional pump model:

$$
J_{\mathrm{pump}} = v_{\mathrm{max}} \frac{[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{i}}^2}{\mathrm{EC}_{50}^2 + [\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{i}}^2}
$$

Where $v_{\mathrm{max}}$ is the maximum pumping rate, and $\mathrm{EC}_{50}$ is the calcium concentration at which the pump operates at half-capacity (typically a very low $\sim 0.3$ µM).

**The problem:** This model ignores the SR completely! It violates the laws of thermodynamics because it predicts SERCA will endlessly pump calcium into the SR even if the SR is already bursting with infinite pressure.

## 2. The bidirectional pump

To fix this, we can model SERCA as a proper bidirectional pump. We treat the forward and reverse modes as separate Michaelis-Menten functions with different affinities:

$$
J_{\mathrm{pump}} = v_{\mathrm{max}} \left[ \frac{\left(\frac{[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{i}}}{\mathrm{EC}_{50}^{\mathrm{fwd}}}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{SR}}}{\mathrm{EC}_{50}^{\mathrm{rev}}}\right)^2}{1 + \left(\frac{[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{i}}}{\mathrm{EC}_{50}^{\mathrm{fwd}}}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{SR}}}{\mathrm{EC}_{50}^{\mathrm{rev}}}\right)^2} \right]
$$

This model is much more physiologically accurate. As the SR load increases, the reverse term naturally cancels out the forward term, automatically shutting the pump down when the concentration gradient becomes too steep.

## 3. Markov models and buffering

Because there are so many SERCA molecules in the cell, they don't just transport calcium—they _buffer_ it. At any given moment, a huge amount of calcium is physically stuck bound to the pump proteins.

To model this, we must separate the process of calcium _binding_ from the process of calcium _transporting_. This requires a multi-state Markov model.

There is no limit to the complexity you can add. If you want to model a heart attack where the cell runs out of ATP, you can expand this into a 6-state or even 12-state Markov model that explicitly tracks the binding and hydrolysis of ATP molecules!
