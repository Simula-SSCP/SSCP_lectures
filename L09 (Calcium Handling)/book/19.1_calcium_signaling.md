# Calcium as a signaling molecule

During an action potential, sodium ($\mathrm{Na}^+$), potassium ($\mathrm{K}^+$), and calcium ($\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$) pass through ion channels in the membrane. For sodium and potassium, these fluxes have relatively small impacts on their total intracellular concentrations (shifting at most a few percent).

For calcium, however, the relative change in concentration is enormous! Cytosolic calcium levels can increase by as much as a _thousandfold_ in a matter of tens of milliseconds. This is not because calcium fluxes are overwhelmingly large in absolute magnitude, but rather because the resting (diastolic) calcium level in a healthy cell is incredibly low—typically around ~100 nM.

This rapid, temporary spike in calcium concentration is known as the **calcium transient**. Because the transient is so drastic, it provides a crystal-clear, high signal-to-noise ratio messenger for the cell.

## Force as a function of the calcium transient

Contraction occurs when free calcium diffuses through the cytosol and binds to organelles known as **myofilaments**. The actual mechanism of force production is described by the _sliding filament theory_, which we will cover in a later module.

Importantly, contraction is _not_ an all-or-nothing response. The strength of contraction is a direct, smooth function of the magnitude of the calcium transient! We can model this graded response using the Hill equation:

## Intracellular structures of the cardiomyocyte

To understand how this transient is formed, we must identify the internal hardware of the cell:

```{figure} ../fig/cardiomyocyte.png
---
width: 600px
name: fig_cardiomyocyte
---
Schematic of the most important calcium handling structures inside the myocyte. *(Figure taken from Zipes and Jalife, 2013)*
```

- **The Sarcolemma:** The outer cell membrane. Calcium enters here via L-type calcium channels and leaves via the $\mathrm{Na}^+/\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$-exchanger (NCX).
- **T-tubules:** Deep invaginations of the sarcolemma that burrow into the cell, ensuring that even the deepest interior volumes have a short physical distance to the outer membrane.
- **The Sarcoplasmic Reticulum (SR):** An internal calcium storage tank. Like the outer membrane, it is a lipid bilayer impermeable to calcium, but it contains its own specialized channels and pumps.
- **Dyads:** Microscopic junctional regions where the SR membrane and the T-tubules lie extremely close together.
- **Myofilaments:** The protein structures that bind calcium and physically contract.
