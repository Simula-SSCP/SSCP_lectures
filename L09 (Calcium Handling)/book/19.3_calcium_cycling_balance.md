# Calcium cycling and balance

The process outlined above repeats for every single beat of the heart. Calcium is _cycled_ back and forth between different domains.

Because all calcium entering the cytosol mixes together, the two efflux proteins—**NCX** and **SERCA**—effectively _compete_ for the same calcium to clear it.

- If SERCA wins the tug-of-war, more calcium is stored in the SR (increasing the **SR load**). A higher SR load means the RyRs will release a much larger transient on the _next_ beat!
- If NCX wins the tug-of-war, calcium leaves the cell entirely, decreasing the SR load.

Because the heart beats continuously, it cannot increase or decrease its SR load forever. The system will spontaneously auto-regulate toward a steady-state balance where the amount of calcium released from the SR exactly equals the amount pumped back in by SERCA:

$$
\int J_{\mathrm{RyR}} \, \mathrm{d}t = \int J_{\mathrm{SERCA}} \, \mathrm{d}t
\qquad \text{and} \qquad
\int J_{\mathrm{LTCC}} \, \mathrm{d}t = \int J_{\mathrm{NCX}} \, \mathrm{d}t
$$

## Species and disease differences

The balance between internal and external calcium cycling drastically alters the cell's behavior.

In species with extremely fast heart rates and short action potentials (like rats and mice), the LTCC channels do not stay open very long. To compensate, they rely heavily on massive internal SR calcium release, meaning their SERCA pumps must be incredibly strong compared to their NCX.

Conversely, larger mammals with slower heart rates (like humans and rabbits) have long action potential plateaus. Their LTCC channels stay open a long time, so they are less reliant on SR stores. In these species, NCX plays a much larger role.

This balance is also disrupted in disease. **Heart failure** is frequently associated with a severe downregulation of SERCA function. As SERCA fails, NCX clears more calcium out of the cell, dropping the SR load and resulting in weak, failing contractions.
