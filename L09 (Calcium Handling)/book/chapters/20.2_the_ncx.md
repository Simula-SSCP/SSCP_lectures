# The sodium-calcium exchanger (NCX)

Let us now turn to the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger, or NCX for short, which is the other major actor responsible for cytosolic calcium decay. Unlike SERCA, which pumps calcium into the SR, NCX pumps it fully out of the cell.

NCX is not an ATPase (or _pump_), but rather an _exchanger_. It manages to push calcium against its concentration gradient by coupling to a co-transport of sodium along its own concentration gradient. The stoichiometry of an exchange cycle is three sodium ions in for each calcium ion transported out. Because calcium ions carry a charge of $2e$ and sodium ions carry a charge of $e$, there is a net transfer of one positive charge into the cell per cycle ($3 \times 1e - 1 \times 2e = +1e$). This makes the NCX **electrogenic** — it generates an electrical current. The NCX is therefore not only important for calcium handling, but also for properly modelling the action potential.

## A simple Michaelis-Menten model

The simplest model we had for SERCA used the Michaelis-Menten equation, modelling the pump activity as increasing with cytosolic calcium, eventually becoming saturated. Experiments show that the NCX current exhibits similar saturation behaviour with calcium. We can therefore propose using a similar model:

```{math}
:label: eq:ncx_mm
I_{\mathrm{NCX}} = v_{\mathrm{max}} \frac{[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{i}}}{\mathrm{EC}_{50} + [\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{i}}}
```

This model captures the dependence on cytosolic calcium and the saturation behaviour quite well, but it has serious limitations: it completely neglects the impact that cytosolic sodium levels and the membrane potential have on the NCX. The fact that there is a potential across the cell membrane, and the co-transport of sodium, makes NCX considerably more complex than SERCA.

To better understand the effect of $V$ and $[\mathrm{Na}^+]_{\mathrm{i}}$ on NCX activity, we can start by analyzing the thermodynamics of the NCX. Please turn to {doc}`Exercise 20.2 <../exercises/exercise_20.2_ncx_thermodynamics>` and work through it now.

## The reversal potential

From the thermodynamic analysis in the exercise, you should have found that the **reversal potential** of the NCX is:

```{math}
:label: eq:ncx_reversal
E_{\mathrm{NCX}} = 3E_{\mathrm{Na}} - 2E_{\mathrm{Ca}}
```

This reversal potential is completely analogous to the Nernst potential of our other passive ionic currents. It is the potential at which NCX activity ceases, because there is no net electrodiffusive driving force. Below this potential, NCX drives calcium out of the cell (forward mode); above it, NCX lets calcium into the cell (reverse mode).

## Why not an Ohmic model?

Because we found a reversal potential analogous to the Nernst potential, it might be tempting to model the NCX as a simple Ohmic current:

```{math}
:label: eq:ncx_ohmic
I_{\mathrm{NCX}} = g_{\mathrm{NCX}}(V - E_{\mathrm{NCX}})
```

However, recall that the Ohmic current was already a rough approximation even for individual ion channels. In practice, the NCX is too complex to approximate in an Ohmic manner, and its kinetics depend strongly on both calcium and sodium concentrations. Nevertheless, the reversal potential analysis tells us _when_ the exchanger is active and _in which direction_ the exchange is occurring, so the thermodynamic analysis was valuable even if it does not give us a direct current expression.

## The Luo-Rudy formulation

A more realistic model of the NCX current was developed by Mullins (1977), simplified by DiFrancesco and Noble (1985), and further modified by Luo and Rudy (1994). Starting from the Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz flux framework, the resulting formulation captures the complex dependencies on voltage, sodium, and calcium concentrations:

```{math}
:label: eq:ncx_luorudy
I_{\mathrm{NaCa}} = k_{\mathrm{NaCa}} \frac{[\mathrm{Na}^+]^3_{\mathrm{i}} [\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{e}} \exp\left[\eta V \frac{F}{RT}\right] - [\mathrm{Na}^+]^3_{\mathrm{e}} [\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{i}} \exp\left[(\eta-1) V \frac{F}{RT}\right]}{\left(K_{\mathrm{m, Na}}^3 + [\mathrm{Na}^+]_{\mathrm{e}}^3\right) \left(K_{\mathrm{m, Ca}} + [\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{e}}\right) \left(1 + k_{\mathrm{sat}} \exp\left[(\eta-1) V \frac{F}{RT}\right]\right)}
```

While this expression is conceptually and computationally heavy compared to a linear model, it does not introduce any new differential states, which is a practical advantage. The model has only five free parameters:

| Parameter | Value | Description |
|---|---|---|
| $k_{\mathrm{NaCa}}$ | 2000 µA/µF | Scaling factor |
| $K_{\mathrm{m,Na}}$ | 87.5 mM | Sodium half-saturation |
| $K_{\mathrm{m,Ca}}$ | 1.38 mM | Calcium half-saturation |
| $k_{\mathrm{sat}}$ | 0.1 | Saturation factor at negative potentials |
| $\eta$ | 0.35 | Position of the energy barrier |

## NCX and afterdepolarizations

The fact that the NCX is electrogenic makes it a prime suspect in **arrhythmogenesis**.

Consider a diseased cell at rest. If the SR spontaneously releases a wave of calcium into the cytosol, the NCX will immediately activate to clear it. However, because it brings 3 Na⁺ ions in for every 1 Ca²⁺ it pumps out, it generates a net _inward_ (depolarizing) current.

If this NCX current is sufficiently strong, it can depolarize the resting membrane past the sodium channel threshold, spontaneously triggering a new action potential. Such events are known as **Delayed Afterdepolarizations (DADs)** and can lead to dangerous cardiac arrhythmias.
