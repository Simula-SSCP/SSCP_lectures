# The SERCA pump

The model we used to explore calcium cycling in Exercise 19.1 is very simple, with linear currents for the calcium influx, efflux, and uptake into the SR. These expressions work well for exploring the system in simple terms, but more complex models can of course be built. We have already discussed L-type Ca²⁺ channels in earlier modules, so let us now take a closer look at the active transport carried by SERCA and the NCX, starting with the former.

The SERCA pump (Sarco/Endoplasmic Reticulum Calcium-ATPase) uses the energy stored in ATP to pump calcium from the cytosol back into the SR. It is one of the two major actors removing calcium from the cytosol at the end of the action potential, and the primary actor refilling the SR.

Because the SR membrane is densely packed with these pumps, SERCA is a **high-capacity** pump. Because it maintains high activity even when cytosolic calcium drops to very low levels, it is also a **high-affinity** pump.

Before we look at the pump models themselves, it is very helpful to analyze the thermodynamic limits of the SERCA pump. Please turn to {doc}`Exercise 20.1 <../exercises/exercise_20.1_serca_thermodynamics>` and work through it now. Analyzing the thermodynamic limits will tell us little about kinetics or rates, but it will give insight into how large calcium gradients we expect the SERCA pump to be able to maintain.

## The pumping reaction

The most basic feature of the SERCA pump is that two calcium ions bind to the pump, get transported from the cytosol into the SR, and then get released. We can write this as the simplified reaction equation:

```{math}
:label: eq:serca_reaction
2\mathrm{Ca_i} + \mathrm{P} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{Ca_2 P} \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{Ca_{SR}} + \mathrm{P}
```

Here $\mathrm{Ca_i}$ represents calcium ions in the cytosol, P represents a free SERCA pump molecule, and $\mathrm{Ca_{SR}}$ represents calcium ions in the SR. There are two things worth noting from this reaction. First, the reaction, in general, _can_ happen in the reverse direction — SERCA is a bidirectional pump. In practice, however, the _net_ action will typically yield a large calcium flux into the SR. Second, the reaction equation effectively states that SERCA pump molecules behave like _enzymes_, as they move calcium without getting used up.

:class: tip
The following discussion of SERCA models is based on [Koivumäki et al., _Modelling sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase and its regulation in cardiac myocytes_. Phil. Trans. R. ## Model 1: The unidirectional pump

Based on our two observations above — (1) ignoring the reverse direction makes the pump _unidirectional_, and (2) the enzyme-like behaviour justifies a Hill-type equation — we can write the simplest model. Because SERCA pumps two ions at a time, we get cooperativity with a Hill coefficient of 2:

If we assume the pump only runs forward (ignoring reverse leaks), we get the unidirectional pump model:


```{math}
:label: eq:serca_uni
J_{\mathrm{pump}} = v_{\mathrm{max}} \frac{[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{i}}^2}{\mathrm{EC}_{50}^2 + [\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{i}}^2}
```

where $v_{\mathrm{max}}$ is the maximum pumping rate, and $\mathrm{EC}_{50}$ is the calcium concentration at which the pump operates at half capacity (typically a very low $\sim 0.3$ µM). Because SERCA is a high-affinity pump, we expect $\mathrm{EC}_{50}$ to be small, meaning that SERCA operates near full capacity even for small elevations above the resting calcium level.

```{figure} ../../fig/serca1.png
---
width: 300px
name: fig_serca1
---
The unidirectional SERCA pump model treats the pump as a simple enzymatic process. *(Figure from Koivumäki et al., 2009)*
```

**The problem:** This model ignores the SR load entirely. It predicts SERCA will continue pumping calcium into the SR indefinitely, regardless of how high the SR concentration becomes. This is not only unphysiological — it violates the laws of thermodynamics. Models using a unidirectional SERCA pump therefore typically require a phenomenological calcium leak current from the SR into the cytosol to compensate.

## Model 2: Unidirectional pump with SR inhibition

To account for the SR load, one can add a damping term that reduces the flux as the SR concentration increases:

```{math}
:label: eq:serca_inhibition
J_{\mathrm{pump}} = v_{\mathrm{max}} \frac{[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{i}}^2}{\mathrm{EC}_{50}^2 + [\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{i}}^2} \times \frac{\mathrm{const.}}{[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{SR}}}
```

```{figure} ../../fig/serca2.png
---
width: 300px
name: fig_serca2
---
The unidirectional SERCA pump model extended with an SR load inhibition term. *(Figure from Koivumäki et al., 2009)*
```

This is _somewhat_ better, as the pump now slows down at high SR loads. However, the model remains fundamentally unphysical: the pump still cannot fully shut off unless $[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{i}} = 0$ or $[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{SR}} = \infty$, and it still cannot transport calcium in the reverse direction.

## Model 3: The bidirectional pump

A more physiologically accurate approach is to model SERCA as a proper bidirectional pump. We treat the forward and reverse modes as separate Michaelis-Menten functions with different affinities:

```{math}
:label: eq:serca_bidir_split
J_{\mathrm{pump}} = J_{\mathrm{fwd}} - J_{\mathrm{rev}}, \quad
J_{\mathrm{fwd}} = v_{\mathrm{max}} \frac{[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{i}}^2}{\mathrm{EC}_{50}^{\mathrm{fwd}\,2} + [\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{i}}^2}, \quad
J_{\mathrm{rev}} = v_{\mathrm{max}} \frac{[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{SR}}^2}{\mathrm{EC}_{50}^{\mathrm{rev}\,2} + [\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{SR}}^2}
```

```{figure} ../../fig/serca3.png
---
width: 300px
name: fig_serca3
---
A bidirectional SERCA pump model, treating the two modes as independent processes. *(Figure from Koivumäki et al., 2009)*
```

These two terms can be combined algebraically into a single compact expression:

```{math}
:label: eq:serca_bidir
J_{\mathrm{pump}} = v_{\mathrm{max}} \left[ \frac{\left(\frac{[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{i}}}{\mathrm{EC}_{50}^{\mathrm{fwd}}}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{SR}}}{\mathrm{EC}_{50}^{\mathrm{rev}}}\right)^2}{1 + \left(\frac{[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{i}}}{\mathrm{EC}_{50}^{\mathrm{fwd}}}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{SR}}}{\mathrm{EC}_{50}^{\mathrm{rev}}}\right)^2} \right]
```

This model is substantially more accurate. In particular, it naturally predicts a maximum concentration ratio at which the pump stalls. Setting $J_{\mathrm{pump}} = 0$:

```{math}
:label: eq:serca_equilibrium
\frac{[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{SR}}}{[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{i}}} = \frac{\mathrm{EC}_{50}^{\mathrm{rev}}}{\mathrm{EC}_{50}^{\mathrm{fwd}}}
```

Thus, by setting the ratio of the two affinities to approximately 1:7000, we recover the experimentally observed maximum gradient that SERCA can maintain — the same value that can be derived from thermodynamic analysis (see {doc}`Exercise 20.1 <../exercises/exercise_20.1_serca_thermodynamics>`)

## Model 4: Markov models and buffering

Because there are so many SERCA molecules in the cell, they do not just transport calcium — they _buffer_ it. At any given moment, a substantial amount of calcium is physically bound to the pump proteins, waiting to be transported. In fact, SERCA is the second largest calcium buffer in the cell.

To capture this effect, we must separate the process of calcium _binding_ from the process of calcium _transporting_. This requires a multi-state Markov model.

```{figure} ../../fig/serca4.png
---
width: 300px
name: fig_serca4
---
A four-state Markov model of the SERCA pump. States $X_2$ and $Y_2$ have calcium physically bound to them, accounting for the pump's buffering capacity. The forward and reverse modes correspond to the clockwise and counter-clockwise cycling of the model. *(Figure from Koivumäki et al., 2009)*
```

This model separates calcium binding from transport by introducing four states. The pump velocity is determined by the _cycling rate_ of the model — the rate at which one full cycle of all four steps occurs. While more accurate, the model introduces 8 free parameters (the forward and reverse rates between the states) and 4 new state variables in the system of ODEs.

## Measuring intracellular calcium

Before we move on, it is worth noting that constraining models of internal channels and pumps such as SERCA and RyR presents significant experimental challenges. Unlike sarcolemmal ion channels, these internal proteins cannot be directly patch-clamped.

One common approach is to use **calcium indicators** — fluorescent molecules that are introduced into the cell and bind calcium, causing them to fluoresce. By measuring the fluorescence with an optical microscope, one can estimate intracellular calcium concentrations. However, the indicators themselves bind calcium, acting as a buffer on the very signal being measured (an example of the _observer effect_). This buffering can be incorporated into computational models for more accurate comparison between simulation and experiment.

Another approach exploits sarcolemmal currents that _can_ be patch-clamped. For example, to measure the **SR load** of a cell, one can add a sufficient concentration of caffeine to trigger the RyR to open completely, releasing all SR calcium into the cytosol. Because the RyR remains open, any calcium pumped back into the SR by SERCA immediately returns to the cytosol. The NCX, however, pumps calcium out of the cell, and this efflux current can be measured by patch clamp. Integrating the NCX current over time yields an estimate of the total calcium that was stored in the SR.

```{figure} ../../fig/measuring_SRload.png
---
width: 600px
name: fig_measuring_sr_load
---
Measuring the SR load by injecting caffeine. The RyR opens fully, and all SR calcium is driven out of the cell by the NCX. Integrating the NCX current provides an estimate of the total SR calcium. *(Figure from Bers, Circulation Research, 2000)*
```

This measurement is destructive — it drains the cell of its calcium and provides only a single data point. Nevertheless, it highlights the importance of computational modeling: because internal dynamics are difficult to measure directly, models become essential tools for reconciling our mechanistic understanding with the limited experimental observations that are available.
