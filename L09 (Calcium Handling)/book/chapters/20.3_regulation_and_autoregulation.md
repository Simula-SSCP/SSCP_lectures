# Regulation and autoregulation

The body has several mechanisms to dynamically regulate excitation-contraction coupling and adjust the force of the heartbeat in response to changing demands.

## Regulation of SERCA by phospholamban

As we discussed earlier, the balance between SERCA and NCX is a critical factor in EC coupling, because this balance determines the SR load, which in turn sets the magnitude of the calcium transient. One direct way to regulate EC coupling is therefore to up- or downregulate SERCA activity.

SERCA is regulated by a molecule called **phospholamban (PLB)**, which inhibits SERCA activity. PLB can be thought of as the "brakes" on the SERCA pump; under normal conditions, these brakes are partially engaged.

```{figure} ../../fig/serca_plb.png
---
width: 300px
name: fig_serca_plb
---
SERCA is directly regulated by phospholamban (PLB). When PLB is present, it inhibits SERCA activity. Up- or downregulating PLB is a major way the cell shifts the EC coupling balance.
```

Two important signaling molecules — **Protein Kinase A (PKA)** and **CaMKII** — can phosphorylate PLB, effectively reducing its inhibitory effect and releasing the brakes on SERCA. As a result, SERCA activity increases, the SR load rises, and the cell produces stronger calcium transients and stronger contractions.

## Beta-adrenergic stimulus (Adrenaline)

Both PKA and CaMKII are involved in several signaling cascades, but perhaps the most important is the **beta-adrenergic response**. When adrenaline (epinephrine) or norepinephrine binds to $\beta$-receptors on the outside of the cardiomyocyte, the intracellular ATP is converted to cyclic AMP (cAMP), which in turn activates PKA and CaMKII, initiating multiple downstream effects:

1. **SERCA upregulation** — PLB is phosphorylated, releasing the brakes on SERCA and allowing faster calcium reuptake into the SR.
2. **L-type calcium channel upregulation** — More trigger calcium enters the cell on each beat.
3. **Increased myofilament calcium sensitivity** — The same calcium transient produces a stronger contraction.

```{figure} ../../fig/beta_adrenergic.png
---
width: 600px
name: fig_beta_adrenergic
---
Schematic of the main targets of $\beta$-adrenergic stimulus (adrenaline) in the cardiomyocyte. PKA and CaMKII upregulate SERCA (via PLB), L-type calcium channels, and myofilament calcium sensitivity.
```

These three changes combine to produce a substantially larger calcium transient and stronger contraction. This is why a healthy heart exhibits a **positive force-frequency relationship** — the faster the heart beats, the harder it squeezes.

```{figure} ../../fig/force_frequency.png
---
width: 400px
name: fig_force_frequency
---
A healthy heart (white circles) has a positive force-frequency relationship. A failing heart (black circles) loses this regulation, leading to a flat or even negative relationship. *(Figure from Bers, Circulation Research, 2000)*
```

In heart failure, SERCA function is often severely downregulated, disrupting the force-frequency relationship. As SERCA weakens, the SR load falls, calcium transients shrink, and the heart can no longer increase its contractile force in response to faster pacing.

## Autoregulation

Cardiomyocytes are, to a certain extent, inherently self-regulating. This property is beautifully illustrated by two complementary experiments.

### The caffeine experiment

In this experiment, a cell is paced at a regular rate and the amount of shortening per beat is measured as a proxy for the strength of contraction. After establishing a baseline, a dose of **caffeine** is added.

Caffeine binds to the RyRs, increasing their calcium sensitivity and making them more likely to open. The immediate result is a larger calcium release, a larger transient, and a stronger contraction.

```{figure} ../../fig/autoregulation0.png
---
width: 500px
name: fig_autoregulation
---
A cardiomyocyte is paced at a regular interval and the resulting cell shortening is measured. When caffeine is added, the contraction is immediately boosted. However, over the next several beats, the cell gradually autoregulates back to baseline. The reverse occurs when caffeine is removed. *(Adapted from Sjaastad et al., 2003)*
```

**Why does the cell return to baseline?** The caffeine increased the RyR-mediated calcium release, but the SERCA/NCX balance was _not_ shifted. The additional calcium released from the SR floods the cytosol, and the NCX — seeing elevated calcium — works overtime to pump it out of the cell entirely. This calcium is now lost from the cell, and SERCA has less to pump back into the SR. On the next beat, the SR load is lower, partially counteracting the sensitizing effect of the caffeine. Over several beats, the system drifts to a new equilibrium where the transient magnitude returns to baseline, albeit at a lower overall SR load.

### The tetracaine experiment

The same group performed a complementary experiment using **tetracaine**, which has the opposite effect of caffeine: it _inhibits_ RyR release.

Immediately after adding tetracaine, the calcium release is much weaker, producing a small transient and minimal shortening. However, over the next 10–20 beats, the cell autoregulates by _increasing_ its SR load. Because less calcium is released from the SR, less is pumped out by the NCX, and SERCA gradually fills the SR to a higher level. Eventually, the larger SR load compensates for the inhibited RyR, and contraction returns to approximately the same baseline.

```{figure} ../../fig/tetracaine.png
---
width: 500px
name: fig_tetracaine
---
Adding tetracaine to the cardiomyocyte inhibits SR calcium release, initially weakening contraction. Over several beats, the SR load increases and the cell autoregulates back to baseline. The lower panels show patch-clamp measurements of the combined L-type and NCX currents, confirming that net calcium flux into the cell is nonzero during the transient period (points b and d), reflecting the changing SR load. *(Adapted from Sjaastad et al., 2003)*
```

The experimentalists also measured the combined L-type and NCX currents via patch clamp. At baseline (points a and c in the figure), the integrated current over one beat goes to zero, confirming that no net calcium enters or leaves the cell. After perturbation (points b and d), the integral is nonzero, indicating a net calcium flux — precisely the change in SR load predicted by the calcium balance model.

These experiments illustrate how the calcium cycling machinery can autoregulate in response to perturbations, lending stability to the system and providing an excellent experimental confirmation of our theoretical understanding of intracellular calcium handling.
