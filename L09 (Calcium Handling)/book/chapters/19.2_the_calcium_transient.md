# The calcium transient, step by step

Let us step through the exact timeline of a single heartbeat, starting at rest, watching the calcium transient explode into the cytosol, and finally decaying back to baseline.

## Step 1: Calcium influx

At rest, cytosolic calcium is very low, while calcium levels outside the cell and inside the SR are very high. When the action potential depolarizes the membrane, **L-type calcium channels (LTCC)** pop open, and calcium flows into the cell.

```{figure} ../../fig/ecc_ltcc.png
---
width: 600px
name: fig_ecc_ltcc
---
At the start of the AP, calcium begins entering through the L-type $\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$ channels.
```

## Step 2: Calcium-induced calcium release (CICR)

The SR contains its own calcium release channels called **Ryanodine Receptors (RyR)**. RyRs cannot sense voltage; instead, they are triggered to open when calcium physically binds to them.

The calcium entering via the LTCCs binds directly to the RyRs in the dyad, triggering a massive, secondary release of calcium from the SR. This explosive positive-feedback mechanism is known as **calcium-induced calcium release (CICR)**. Under normal conditions, the calcium flux from the SR is much larger than the initial influx from outside the cell.

```{figure} ../../fig/ecc_cicr.png
---
width: 600px
name: fig_ecc_cicr
---
Following influx through L-type channels, a massive secondary calcium release from the SR through RyR channels is triggered.
```

## Step 3: Diffusion and contraction

This massive influx of calcium diffuses out of the dyads and into the bulk cytosol. As it spreads, it dynamically binds and unbinds to myofilaments, triggering mechanical contraction. The higher the cytosolic calcium levels rise, the stronger the contraction becomes.

```{figure} ../../fig/ecc_buffering.png
---
width: 600px
name: fig_ecc_buffering
---
Calcium diffuses into the bulk cytosol and binds to myofilaments, causing them to contract.
```

## Step 4: Decay back to baseline

To allow the heart to relax and refill with blood, the calcium must be cleared from the cytosol. Because it must be pushed _against_ its concentration gradient, active transport is required.

- **The SERCA pump** uses ATP to actively pump calcium back into the SR, replenishing the internal stores.
- **The NCX exchanger** pumps calcium entirely out of the cell by coupling it to an influx of sodium.

```{figure} ../../fig/ecc_decay.png
---
width: 600px
name: fig_ecc_decay
---
At the end of the action potential, calcium is removed from the cytosol by SERCA (into the SR) and NCX (out of the cell) to ensure relaxation.
```
