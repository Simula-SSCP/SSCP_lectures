# Calcium as a signaling molecule

During an action potential, sodium ($\mathrm{Na}^+$), potassium ($\mathrm{K}^+$), and calcium ($\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$) pass through ion channels in the membrane. For sodium and potassium, these fluxes have relatively small impacts on their total intracellular concentrations (shifting at most a few percent).

For calcium, however, the relative change in concentration can be enormous. Cytosolic calcium levels can increase by as much as a _thousandfold_ in a matter of tens of milliseconds. This is not because calcium fluxes are overwhelmingly large in absolute magnitude, but rather because the resting (diastolic) calcium level in a healthy cell is incredibly low—typically around ~100 nM.

This rapid, temporary spike in calcium concentration is known as the **calcium transient**. Because the transient is so drastic, it provides a high signal-to-noise ratio messenger for the cell. In comparison, sodium and potassium levels undergo such small relative changes that they carry poor signal quality.

In addition to triggering contraction, calcium is a signal for a large number of processes in the cell, both short-term and long-term. In the short term, elevated calcium levels can drive the activity of kinases, phosphatases, ion channels, exchangers, and transporters. In the long term, sustained or repeated calcium elevations can influence cell growth, gene expression, differentiation, remodeling, and development.

In chronic heart disease, calcium handling is often found to be disrupted. Understanding calcium dynamics is also crucial when using animal models or developing drugs, as the calcium handling balance differs substantially across mammalian species.

## Force as a function of the calcium transient

Contraction occurs when free calcium diffuses through the cytosol and binds to organelles known as **myofilaments**, causing contraction. The mechanism of force production in the myofilaments is described by _sliding filament theory_, which is the topic of a later module. For now, we simply note that free calcium causes contraction, and that contraction ends once calcium levels drop back down.

Importantly, contraction is _not_ an all-or-nothing response. Rather, the strength of contraction is a direct function of the _magnitude_ of the calcium transient. We can model this using the Hill equation with a Hill coefficient of $n=4$ and a half-saturation point of $K_{1/2} = 600$ nM free calcium. A coefficient of 4 means force production is sigmoidal, with a steep dependence on the calcium transient magnitude.

## Intracellular structures of the cardiomyocyte

To understand how the calcium transient is formed, it helps to have an overview of the most important structures of the cell:

```{figure} ../../fig/cardiomyocyte.png
---
width: 600px
name: fig_cardiomyocyte
---
Schematic of some of the most important calcium handling structures inside the myocyte. *(Figure from Zipes and Jalife, 2013)*
```

- **The Sarcolemma:** The outer cell membrane. During an action potential, calcium enters through calcium channels (primarily the L-type channel). Calcium efflux is primarily driven by the $\mathrm{Na}^+/\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$-exchanger (NCX) and the plasma membrane $\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$-pump (PMCA).
- **T-tubules:** Deep invaginations of the sarcolemma into the cell, giving the cell a much larger surface area and ensuring most of the internal volume has a short distance to the membrane.
- **Myofilaments:** The protein structures that contract and produce force when activated by cytosolic calcium.
- **Mitochondria:** The powerhouse of the cell, producing the ATP required for contraction. Mitochondria do absorb and extrude a small amount of $\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$, but these quantities are small and usually ignored in models.
- **The Sarcoplasmic Reticulum (SR):** An internal calcium storage. The SR consists of a lipid bilayer membrane impermeable to $\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$, like the external cell membrane, and contains its own specialized ion channels and pumps for calcium.
- **Dyads:** Regions where the SR membrane and the T-tubules lie close together, especially important for calcium release through calcium-induced calcium release (CICR).
