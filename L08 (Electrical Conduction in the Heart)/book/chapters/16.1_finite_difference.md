# Introduction to finite difference methods

Finite difference methods (FDM) are numerical techniques for approximating solutions to differential equations by replacing continuous derivatives with discrete approximations. They form the foundation of computational mathematics and are essential tools for solving the partial differential equations (PDEs) that arise in physics, engineering, and — as we shall see — computational biology.

The core principle is **discretization**: instead of working with a continuous physical cable and continuous time, we slice both space and time into a finite grid of discrete points. We then approximate the voltage solution at these specific grid points, transforming the differential equation into a system of algebraic equations that can be solved computationally.

## The discretization process

The discretization process involves two key steps:

1. **Spatial discretization:** The continuous spatial domain (a cable of length $L$) is divided into a finite number of grid points separated by a uniform spacing $\Delta x$.
2. **Temporal discretization:** Time is advanced forward in discrete steps of size $\Delta t$.

At each grid point $(x_i, t_n)$, we approximate the true continuous solution $V(x_i, t_n)$ with a discrete variable $V_i^n$, where:

- $i$ is the spatial index: $x_i = i \cdot \Delta x$ for $i = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, N_x$
- $n$ is the temporal index: $t_n = n \cdot \Delta t$ for $n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, N_t$

## Derivative approximations

The fundamental idea of FDM is to approximate continuous derivatives using Taylor series expansions evaluated at neighbouring grid points. For a smooth function $f(x)$, the Taylor expansion about $x_i$ gives:

```{math}
:label: eq:taylor_forward
f(x_i + \Delta x) = f(x_i) + \Delta x \, f'(x_i) + \frac{(\Delta x)^2}{2} f''(x_i) + \mathcal{O}((\Delta x)^3)
```

```{math}
:label: eq:taylor_backward
f(x_i - \Delta x) = f(x_i) - \Delta x \, f'(x_i) + \frac{(\Delta x)^2}{2} f''(x_i) - \mathcal{O}((\Delta x)^3)
```

By manipulating these expansions, we can isolate the first and second derivatives.

### First derivative approximations

Three standard approximations for the first derivative are:

**Forward difference** (first-order accurate):

```{math}
:label: eq:forward_diff
\frac{df}{dx}\bigg|_{x_i} \approx \frac{f_{i+1} - f_i}{\Delta x} + \mathcal{O}(\Delta x)
```

**Backward difference** (first-order accurate):

```{math}
:label: eq:backward_diff
\frac{df}{dx}\bigg|_{x_i} \approx \frac{f_i - f_{i-1}}{\Delta x} + \mathcal{O}(\Delta x)
```

**Central difference** (second-order accurate):

```{math}
:label: eq:central_diff_first
\frac{df}{dx}\bigg|_{x_i} \approx \frac{f_{i+1} - f_{i-1}}{2\Delta x} + \mathcal{O}((\Delta x)^2)
```

The central difference achieves higher accuracy because it symmetrically balances information from both sides of the point, causing the leading-order error terms to cancel.

### Second derivative approximation

Adding the two Taylor expansions ({eq}`eq:taylor_forward` and {eq}`eq:taylor_backward`) and rearranging yields the standard central difference formula for the second derivative:

```{math}
:label: eq:central_diff_second
\frac{d^2 f}{dx^2}\bigg|_{x_i} \approx \frac{f_{i+1} - 2f_i + f_{i-1}}{(\Delta x)^2} + \mathcal{O}((\Delta x)^2)
```

This is the formula we will use to discretize the spatial diffusion term ($\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2}$) of the cable equation.

### Truncation error and accuracy

The $\mathcal{O}(\Delta x)$ and $\mathcal{O}((\Delta x)^2)$ terms in the approximations above represent the **truncation error** — the difference between the exact derivative and its finite difference approximation. The order of accuracy is determined by the leading power of $\Delta x$ that remains after the Taylor series is truncated.

A first-order scheme ($\mathcal{O}(\Delta x)$) means that halving the grid spacing roughly halves the error. A second-order scheme ($\mathcal{O}((\Delta x)^2)$) means that halving the grid spacing reduces the error by a factor of four. This makes central difference schemes strongly preferred for spatial derivatives whenever possible.

## Explicit update schemes

We now have all the tools to discretize our cable equation. Recall the general reaction-diffusion form:

```{math}
:label: eq:reaction_diffusion
\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = k \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2} + f(V)
```

We discretize the time derivative using a **forward difference** (Forward Euler method) and the spatial derivative using the **central difference** for the second derivative:

```{math}
:label: eq:explicit_discretized
\frac{V_i^{n+1} - V_i^n}{\Delta t} = k \frac{V_{i+1}^n - 2V_i^n + V_{i-1}^n}{(\Delta x)^2} + f(V_i^n)
```

The crucial observation is that the only unknown in this entire equation is $V_i^{n+1}$ — the voltage at the next time step. Because all other values ($V_i^n$, $V_{i-1}^n$, $V_{i+1}^n$) are known from the current time step, we can rearrange to solve directly:

```{math}
:label: eq:explicit_update
V_i^{n+1} = V_i^n + \Delta t \left[ k \frac{V_{i+1}^n - 2V_i^n + V_{i-1}^n}{(\Delta x)^2} + f(V_i^n) \right]
```

This is called an **explicit update scheme**: the future is computed directly from the present, with no system of equations to solve. Each spatial grid point can be updated independently, making the method simple to implement and naturally parallelisable.

## Explicit vs. implicit schemes

An alternative approach is the **implicit scheme**, where the spatial derivatives are evaluated at the _future_ time level ($n+1$) rather than the current level ($n$). This leads to a system of coupled equations that must be solved simultaneously at each time step (typically by matrix inversion).

```{figure} ../../figs/explicit_implicit_comparison.png
Comparison of explicit and implicit stencils. **Left:** In the explicit scheme, the unknown value (red circle) at time level $n+1$ is computed directly from known values (blue squares) at time level $n$. **Right:** In the implicit scheme, the spatial derivative is evaluated at the unknown time level $n+1$, coupling all unknown values (red circles) together and requiring a linear system solve.
```

The key trade-off between the two approaches:

| Property | Explicit | Implicit |
|----------|----------|----------|
| Implementation | Simple — direct formula | Complex — requires solving a linear system |
| Cost per time step | Low | Higher |
| Stability | Conditionally stable — requires small $\Delta t$ | Unconditionally stable |

For the exercises in this module, we will use the explicit scheme exclusively, as it is simpler to implement and sufficient for our purposes.

## Stability of the explicit scheme

The explicit scheme has one critical limitation: it is only **conditionally stable**. If the time step $\Delta t$ is too large relative to the spatial spacing $\Delta x$, the numerical solution will develop rapidly growing oscillations and blow up to infinity.

For the pure diffusion equation ($f = 0$), the stability condition is:

```{math}
:label: eq:stability_condition
r = \frac{k \Delta t}{(\Delta x)^2} \leq \frac{1}{2}
```

where $r$ is the dimensionless **stability parameter** (also known as the mesh ratio or Courant number for diffusion). If $r$ exceeds $\frac{1}{2}$, the scheme is unstable and the solution will diverge.

```{figure} ../../figs/stability_analysis.png
---
width: 700px
name: fig_stability
---
**Left:** The stability boundary at $r = 0.5$. For $r \leq 0.5$ the scheme is stable; for $r > 0.5$ it is unstable. **Right:** A demonstration of the effect — a stable solution ($r = 0.4$, green) correctly decays to zero, while an unstable solution ($r = 0.6$, red) develops catastrophic oscillations.
```

In practice, this means that if we want a fine spatial grid (small $\Delta x$), we must use an even finer time step ($\Delta t \leq \frac{(\Delta x)^2}{2k}$). This quadratic relationship between $\Delta x$ and $\Delta t$ can make explicit methods computationally expensive for very fine grids.

:::{admonition} Practical tip
:class: tip

When running the exercises in this module, if your simulation produces values that rapidly grow to enormous magnitudes or oscillate wildly, the most likely cause is a violated stability condition. Try reducing $\Delta t$ or increasing $\Delta x$.
:::
