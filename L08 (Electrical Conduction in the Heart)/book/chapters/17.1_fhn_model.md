# The FitzHugh-Nagumo model

## From bistable to excitable

The bistable equation perfectly describes a propagating _front_, but because it has only one variable, the voltage can only transition from 0 to 1 — it never returns to rest. A real action potential, of course, repolarises: the membrane returns to its resting potential and becomes ready to fire again.

To capture this, we need a second variable that slowly pulls the voltage back down. The result is the **FitzHugh-Nagumo (FHN) model**, the simplest mathematical model that reproduces the qualitative features of excitable tissue: a threshold, a rapid upstroke, repolarisation, and a refractory period.

## Historical context

The FHN model was developed independently by two researchers in the early 1960s:

- **Richard FitzHugh** (1961), working at the National Institutes of Health (NIH), sought a simplified version of the Hodgkin-Huxley model that would make its essential dynamics mathematically transparent. He reduced the four HH variables to just two by observing that the fast sodium activation ($m$) and slow sodium inactivation ($h$) could be lumped together with the potassium activation ($n$) into a single recovery variable.
- **Jin-ichi Nagumo** (1962) independently constructed an electronic circuit consisting of a tunnel diode and an inductor that exhibited the same dynamics. The circuit provided a physical analogue of the equations.

The model is sometimes called the **Bonhoeffer-van der Pol model**, since FitzHugh's construction was inspired by the van der Pol oscillator from nonlinear circuit theory.

## The model equations

The FHN model consists of two coupled equations:

```{math}
:label: eq:fhn_voltage
\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = k \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2} + A V(1 - V)(V - \alpha) - w
```

```{math}
:label: eq:fhn_recovery
\frac{\partial w}{\partial t} = \epsilon (V - \gamma w)
```

where:

- $V$ is the membrane voltage (the **fast** variable, analogous to the Hodgkin-Huxley sodium activation).
- $w$ is the **recovery variable** (the **slow** variable, combining the effects of potassium activation and sodium inactivation).

### Parameter interpretation

The parameters $\epsilon$ and $\gamma$ have specific physical meanings:

| Parameter | Typical value | Role |
|-----------|--------------|------|
| $\epsilon$ | 0.005 | Controls the **time scale separation** between $V$ and $w$. Because $\epsilon \ll 1$, the recovery variable $w$ evolves much more slowly than the voltage $V$. This is what creates the characteristic shape of the action potential: fast upstroke, slow recovery. |
| $\gamma$ | 2.0 | Controls the **decay rate** of the recovery variable. It determines how quickly $w$ returns to zero after the voltage repolarises, which in turn sets the duration of the refractory period. |

## Phase plane analysis

To build intuition for the FHN dynamics, it is instructive to temporarily ignore spatial diffusion and examine the local ODE system:

```{math}
:label: eq:fhn_ode_v
\frac{dV}{dt} = AV(1-V)(V-\alpha) - w
```

```{math}
:label: eq:fhn_ode_w
\frac{dw}{dt} = \epsilon(V - \gamma w)
```

The dynamics of this system can be understood by examining its **nullclines** — the curves in the $(V, w)$ plane along which one of the derivatives is zero:

- **$V$-nullcline** ($\frac{dV}{dt} = 0$): This is the cubic curve $w = AV(1-V)(V-\alpha)$. Above this curve, $\frac{dV}{dt} < 0$ (voltage decreases); below it, $\frac{dV}{dt} > 0$ (voltage increases).
- **$w$-nullcline** ($\frac{dw}{dt} = 0$): This is the straight line $w = V/\gamma$. Above this line, $\frac{dw}{dt} < 0$ (recovery decreases); below it, $\frac{dw}{dt} > 0$ (recovery increases).

The intersection of the two nullclines is the **resting equilibrium** of the system (near $V = 0$, $w = 0$ for standard parameter values).

### The excitation cycle

Starting from rest, the following sequence of events produces an action potential:

1. **Stimulus and upstroke:** A perturbation pushes $V$ above the threshold $\alpha$. The system is now below the cubic $V$-nullcline, so $\frac{dV}{dt} > 0$. Because $\epsilon$ is very small, $w$ barely changes during this fast phase, and $V$ rapidly shoots up to the right branch of the cubic (near $V \approx 1$).

2. **Plateau and slow recovery:** With $V$ large, $\frac{dw}{dt} = \epsilon(V - \gamma w) > 0$, so $w$ begins to grow — but slowly, because $\epsilon$ is small. The system drifts upward along the right branch of the cubic nullcline.

3. **Repolarisation:** Eventually, $w$ grows large enough that the operating point moves past the peak of the cubic nullcline. At this point, there is no nearby stable branch, and $V$ rapidly drops back toward 0. This is the repolarisation phase.

4. **Recovery:** With $V$ now small, $\frac{dw}{dt} < 0$, and $w$ slowly decays back toward zero. The system returns to the resting equilibrium, completing the cycle.

In a spatially extended cable, this local excitation cycle propagates as a **travelling pulse**: a wavefront of depolarisation followed by a waveback of repolarisation, with a region of elevated $w$ (the refractory tail) behind it.

## The refractory period

A crucial consequence of the recovery variable is the **refractory period**. Immediately after the passage of an action potential, the recovery variable $w$ is still elevated. This means that the $-w$ term in the voltage equation acts as a strong inhibitory current, preventing the tissue from firing again even if it receives another stimulus.

Only after $w$ has decayed sufficiently close to zero can the tissue be re-excited. This creates a natural minimum time interval between successive action potentials, which has profound consequences:

- It prevents the action potential from triggering itself backwards (ensuring unidirectional propagation).
- It limits the maximum firing rate of the tissue.
- It determines whether reentrant circuits can be sustained, as we shall see next.

## Reentry: self-sustaining circuits

One of the most clinically important phenomena in cardiac electrophysiology is **reentry** — a self-sustaining loop of electrical activation that circulates repeatedly through the tissue. Reentry is the mechanism underlying many dangerous cardiac arrhythmias, including atrial flutter, ventricular tachycardia, and (in more complex, three-dimensional forms) ventricular fibrillation.

### Modelling reentry with periodic boundary conditions

In a straight cable with sealed (Neumann) boundary conditions, a travelling pulse simply reaches the end of the cable and stops. To model a circular loop of tissue — such as a ring of cardiac muscle around an anatomical obstacle — we can replace the Neumann conditions with **periodic boundary conditions**:

```{math}
:label: eq:periodic_bc
v(0, t) = v(L, t)
```

This effectively bends the cable into a ring by connecting the two ends. In the finite difference scheme, this is implemented by modifying the index arrays so that the neighbour to the right of point $N$ is point $0$, and the neighbour to the left of point $0$ is point $N$.

### When does reentry occur?

A single stimulus in the centre of the ring will generate two pulses travelling in opposite directions. When these two wavefronts meet on the opposite side, they collide and annihilate each other (because each wavefront runs into the refractory tail of the other). This is the normal, healthy outcome.

However, if one side of the ring is initially refractory (for example, because a previous stimulus has just passed through), only one of the two wavefronts can propagate. The other is blocked by the elevated recovery variable. The surviving wavefront then travels around the entire ring and, upon returning to the starting point, finds that the tissue has had time to recover. It can therefore re-enter and excite the tissue again, creating a self-sustaining loop.

This is the essence of reentry: **unidirectional block + a circuit long enough for the tissue to recover** = a perpetually circulating activation wave.
