# Introduction to finite difference methods

Finite difference methods (FDM) form the foundation of computational mathematics. They are used to approximate solutions to differential equations by replacing continuous, infinitely small derivatives with discrete, measurable steps.

The core principle behind FDM is **discretization**. Instead of working with a continuous physical cable and continuous time, we slice both space and time into a grid. We approximate the voltage solution at these specific grid points, transforming the complex differential equation into a simple system of algebraic equations.

## The Discretization Process

We break the problem down into two grids:

1. **Spatial Discretization:** The physical cable of length $L$ is divided into a finite number of grid points separated by a physical spacing $\Delta x$.
2. **Temporal Discretization:** Time is advanced forward in discrete steps of size $\Delta t$.

At any specific grid point $(x_i, t_n)$, we approximate the true continuous voltage $V(x_i, t_n)$ with a discrete variable $V_i^n$, where:

- $i$ represents the spatial index: $x_i = i \cdot \Delta x$
- $n$ represents the temporal index: $t_n = n \cdot \Delta t$

## Approximating the Derivatives

The fundamental trick of FDM is approximating continuous derivatives using Taylor series expansions of neighboring grid points.

### The Time Derivative (Forward Euler)

To estimate how fast the voltage is changing in time at our current grid point, we use a **Forward Difference**. We look at the voltage _now_ ($n$) and the voltage one step in the _future_ ($n+1$):

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} \approx \frac{V_i^{n+1} - V_i^n}{\Delta t}
$$

### The Spatial Derivative (Central Difference)

To estimate the spatial curvature of the voltage (the second derivative $\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2}$ from our cable equation), we look at the voltage at our current point ($i$) and compare it to our immediate neighbors to the left ($i-1$) and right ($i+1$):

$$
\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2} \approx \frac{V_{i+1}^n - 2V_i^n + V_{i-1}^n}{(\Delta x)^2}
$$

This is known as a **Central Difference** scheme, and it is the standard approach for spatial derivatives because it provides high accuracy by balancing the information from both sides of the point.

## Explicit Update Schemes

By plugging these two discrete approximations back into a generic reaction-diffusion equation ($\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = k \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2} + f(V)$), we get:

$$
\frac{V_i^{n+1} - V_i^n}{\Delta t} = k \frac{V_{i+1}^n - 2V_i^n + V_{i-1}^n}{(\Delta x)^2} + f(V_i^n)
$$

Notice that the only unknown in this entire equation is $V_i^{n+1}$ (the voltage at the next time step). Because we know all the voltages at our current time step $n$, we can simply rearrange the algebra to solve directly for the future!

$$
V_i^{n+1} = V_i^n + \Delta t \left[ k \frac{V_{i+1}^n - 2V_i^n + V_{i-1}^n}{(\Delta x)^2} + f(V_i^n) \right]
$$

This is called an **explicit update scheme**. It is mathematically elegant and incredibly easy to code: to find the future state of the cable, we simply calculate this formula for every single spatial point $i$, and then step forward in time.
