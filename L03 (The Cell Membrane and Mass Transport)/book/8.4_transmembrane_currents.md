# Transmembrane currents

We have now computed the Nernst potentials for three different ionic species. These equilibrium potentials turn out to be drastically different from one another. If we imagine we have three different cells, each permeable to only _one_ of these ion types, each cell would settle exactly at its respective Nernst potential.

The cells permeable exclusively to sodium and calcium would achieve highly positive potentials (+70 mV and +114 mV). The cell permeable exclusively to potassium would achieve a highly negative potential (-86 mV).

Looking at the concentrations, this makes perfect physical sense. Sodium and calcium have massive _inward_ concentration gradients; these ions are physically driven to enter the cell, which dumps positive charge inside and raises the membrane potential. Because of this, we refer to sodium and calcium currents as **inward currents**. Conversely, potassium is vastly more abundant inside the cell and wants to leave, which drains positive charge and lowers the potential. Potassium generates an **outward current**.

**Left:** Massive differences in ion concentrations create large diffusive "pressures." For Na$^+$ and Ca$^{2+}$, this pressure drives inward currents. For K$^+$, it drives an outward current. <br>
**Right:** The Nernst equilibrium for the three species. If $V = E_{\mathrm{X}}$, the electrical and diffusive driving forces cancel out completely.

## The Nernst potential as a driving force

The Nernst potential tells us when there is zero net flux for a specific ion. If $V = E_{\mathrm{Na}}$, there is no net **driving force** for sodium to cross the membrane. But what happens if the cell's membrane potential is _different_ from the Nernst potential?

If $V \neq E_{\mathrm{Na}}$, there will be an unbalanced electrodiffusive force driving sodium into or out of the cell. This resulting sodium _current_ will continuously shift the membrane potential, pulling it toward $E_{\mathrm{Na}}$.

- If $V < E_{\mathrm{Na}}$, there is a net _influx_ of sodium, driving the voltage up.
- If $V > E_{\mathrm{Na}}$, sodium is forced to _leave_ the cell. This efflux happens even though the sodium concentration is much lower inside, because the immense electrical field completely overpowers the diffusive pressure!

Because the current direction flips depending on whether $V$ is above or below the Nernst potential, the Nernst potential is frequently referred to in electrophysiology as the **reversal potential**.

## Finding an expression for the ion channel current

We know the potential at which the current stops and reverses, but how _strong_ will the current be as a function of the voltage?

What is clear is that the further away the voltage is from equilibrium, the stronger the driving force will be. We therefore want our mathematical current to be a function of the difference $(V - E_{\mathrm{X}})$. We call this difference the _driving force_.

A simple first-order approximation is to assume the current is strictly proportional to this driving force. The proportionality constant is the _conductance_ ($g$), giving us:

```{math}
:label: eq:ohmic_current
I_{\mathrm{Na}} = g_{\mathrm{Na}} (V - E_{\mathrm{Na}})
```

The conductance $g_{\mathrm{Na}}$ is a parameter that captures _how permeable_ the membrane currently is to sodium. It represents how many physical channels are open in the membrane (we will cover this deeply when we model gating).

This first-order approximation is literally Ohm's law! In basic electronics, Ohm's law states that current is proportional to voltage divided by resistance ($I = V/R$). Conductance is simply the inverse of resistance ($g = 1/R$). It makes mathematical sense to use conductance for biology because if all channels close, the conductance smoothly goes to 0, whereas the resistance would explode to infinity. The other key biological modification is that we replaced the purely electrical potential $V$ with the full electrodiffusive driving force $(V - E_{\mathrm{Na}})$.

## How good is the Ohmic approximation?

We didn't derive the Ohmic current from first principles; we simply assumed linearity. Is there a more accurate expression?

Yes. Returning to the full Nernst-Planck equation and solving it strictly through the physical pore of the channel yields the **Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz (GHK) flux equation**. We will show it here, though you do not need to memorize it (note that this equation gives the current density $\Phi$ instead of the total current $I$):

```{math}
:label: eq:ghk_flux
\Phi_{\mathrm{X}} = \frac{P_{\mathrm{X}} z_{\mathrm{X}}^2 F^2}{R T} V \frac{[\mathrm{X}]_{\mathrm{i}} - [\mathrm{X}]_{\mathrm{e}} \exp\left(-\frac{z_{\mathrm{X}} F}{R T} V\right)}{1 - \exp\left(-\frac{z_{\mathrm{X}} F}{R T} V\right)}
```

Comparing the simple Ohmic model to the complex GHK equation, they both agree that current is zero when $V = E_{\mathrm{X}}$. They both agree the current changes direction at this reversal potential. However, they _disagree_ on how quickly the current scales. While the Ohmic current is a perfectly straight line, the GHK equation is nonlinear and exhibits **rectification** (meaning it passes current more easily in one direction than the other).

In massive action potential models containing dozens of different ion channels, it is standard practice to use simple Ohmic expressions for almost all of them to save computational power.

## Writing out our full membrane ODE

Using our Ohmic current approximations, we can now assemble our final ODE to model how mass transport affects the membrane potential over time.

The cell membrane is embedded with a vast number of ion channels. While each channel conducts only a tiny amount of current, we can lump identical channels together and treat them as a single macro-electrical component. We model each ionic species as a conductance coupled in series to a battery (the Nernst potential). We then place all of these ion channel components in _parallel_ to the lipid bilayer capacitor itself.

```{figure} ../fig/ohmic_currents.png
---
width: 600px
name: fig_ohmic_circuit
---
**Circuit-equivalent diagrams for a cell membrane.** <br>
**Left:** A cell with only sodium channels. <br>
**Right:** A cell with sodium, calcium, and potassium channels all coupled in parallel to the membrane capacitor.
```

If we consider a cell permeable _only_ to sodium (the left circuit), the total current entering or leaving the physical system must be zero to prevent an unsustainable build-up of uncompensated charge:

```{math}
:label: eq:current_balance_na
I_{\mathrm{tot}} = I_{\mathrm{cap}} + I_{\mathrm{Na}} = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad I_{\mathrm{cap}} = -I_{\mathrm{Na}}
```

Recall from the previous chapter our ODE for the membrane potential:

```{math}
:label: eq:membrane_ode_cap
\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{m}}} I_{\mathrm{cap}} = -\frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{m}}} I_{\mathrm{Na}}
```

Inserting our Ohmic current gives the final ODE for a sodium-only cell:

```{math}
:label: eq:ode_na_only
\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{m}}} g_{\mathrm{Na}} (V - E_{\mathrm{Na}})
```

### ODE for a cell permeable to multiple ions

If we want to include sodium, potassium, and calcium simultaneously (the right circuit), we simply sum all the ionic currents together:

```{math}
:label: eq:current_balance_all
I_{\mathrm{cap}} = - (I_{\mathrm{Na}} + I_{\mathrm{K}} + I_{\mathrm{Ca}})
```

Which gives us our master ODE for the membrane potential:

```{math}
:label: eq:ode_master
\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{m}}} \Big[ g_{\mathrm{Na}}(V - E_{\mathrm{Na}}) + g_{\mathrm{K}}(V - E_{\mathrm{K}}) + g_{\mathrm{Ca}}(V - E_{\mathrm{Ca}}) \Big]
```
