# The Nernst potential

While solving the Nernst-Planck equation directly is challenging, it clearly tells us that the passive transport of ions is driven by the sum of two independent forces: diffusion and electricity. These forces can point in the same direction, or they can oppose each other. If they oppose each other, they will naturally tend toward an equilibrium situation where they perfectly balance out. Let us imagine a small thought experiment to see why.

Assume we have a mixture of positively charged K$^+$ ions and negatively charged Cl$^-$ ions. There are equal amounts of both, so the mixture is electrically neutral overall. Now, we place a high concentration of this mixture inside the cell and a low concentration outside the cell. Both sides of the membrane are electrically neutral; as a result, there will be absolutely no membrane potential ($V=0$). However, because the concentration is higher inside than outside, there is a massive concentration gradient across the membrane.

Assume now that potassium-specific ion channels in the membrane open up. K$^+$ ions are now free to move, but Cl$^-$ ions remain trapped. Because there is a large concentration gradient but no initial electric potential, the Nernst-Planck equation temporarily reduces to Fick's law, and potassium ions will begin to diffuse _out_ of the cell.

When potassium ions leave the cell, they leave behind a surplus of negatively charged Cl$^-$ ions that cannot follow them. This means that as K$^+$ ions leave, the inside of the cell becomes negatively charged relative to the outside. An electric field starts to form across the membrane, pulling _back_ on the positively charged K$^+$ ions and inhibiting them from leaving.

Eventually, this electric field becomes strong enough to completely cancel out the diffusive pressure, and the system reaches a steady equilibrium. There will still be a concentration gradient pushing ions out, and an electric potential pulling ions in, but these two forces perfectly cancel each other out.

## Deriving the equilibrium potential

The exact membrane potential at which the electric field perfectly balances the diffusive pressure is called the _Nernst potential_ (or simply the _equilibrium potential_). We can derive an expression for the Nernst potential directly from the Nernst-Planck equation.

At absolute equilibrium, there should be zero net flux ($J_k = 0$):

```{math}
:label: eq:np_equilibrium
-D_k\left(\nabla c_k + \frac{z_k F}{R T}c_k \nabla V\right) = 0
```

First, we can divide out the diffusion coefficient $D_k$. Apparently, the _rate_ at which diffusion happens won't affect the physical conditions for _if_ it happens. This leaves:

```{math}
:label: eq:np_simplified
\nabla c = -\frac{z F}{R T} c \nabla V
```

To make sense of this equation spatially, we assume the outside and inside of the cell are perfectly well-mixed. Therefore, gradients only exist directly _across_ the thickness of the membrane itself.

```{figure} ../fig/nernst.png
---
width: 400px
name: fig_nernst_gradients
---
We assume the intracellular and extracellular domains are well-mixed. The concentration $c$ and potential $V$ therefore only change *through* the membrane itself.
```

Because the gradients point straight through the membrane, we can reduce the equation to one spatial dimension ($x$):

```{math}
:label: eq:np_1d
\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}x} = -\frac{z F}{R T} c \frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}x}
```

To find an expression for the potential $V$, we integrate this equation across the thickness of the membrane (from $0$ to $L$):

```{math}
:label: eq:np_integral
\int_0^L \frac{1}{c} \frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}x} \, \mathrm{d}x = -\frac{z F}{R T} \int_0^L \frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}x} \, \mathrm{d}x
```

With a simple substitution of variables, this simplifies to:

```{math}
:label: eq:np_substituted
\int_{c_{\mathrm{i}}}^{c_{\mathrm{e}}} \frac{1}{c} \, \mathrm{d}c = -\frac{z F}{R T} \int_{V_{\mathrm{i}}}^{V_{\mathrm{e}}} \mathrm{d}V
```

Evaluating the integrals gives:

```{math}
:label: eq:np_evaluated
\ln c_{\mathrm{e}} - \ln c_{\mathrm{i}} = -\frac{z F}{R T}(V_{\mathrm{e}} - V_{\mathrm{i}})
```

Recalling our definition that the membrane potential is $V = V_{\mathrm{i}} - V_{\mathrm{e}}$, we can absorb the negative sign and solve for the equilibrium potential $V_{\mathrm{eq}}$:

```{math}
:label: eq:nernst_equation
V_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{R T}{z F} \ln \frac{c_{\mathrm{e}}}{c_{\mathrm{i}}}
```

This is the exact membrane potential where the electrical forces and diffusive pressure perfectly balance. If the cell sits at $V = V_{\mathrm{eq}}$, there will be zero net transport of ions across the membrane, even if every channel is wide open. This specific potential is known as the **Nernst potential**.

Because the Nernst-Planck equation is species-specific, so is the resulting potential. It is almost always denoted as $E_{\mathrm{X}}$ for a given ionic species $\mathrm{X}$:

```{math}
:label: eq:nernst_species
E_{\mathrm{X}} = \frac{R T}{z_{\mathrm{X}} F} \ln \frac{[\mathrm{X}]_{\mathrm{e}}}{[\mathrm{X}]_{\mathrm{i}}}
```

For example, the Nernst potentials for sodium, potassium, and calcium are denoted as $E_{\mathrm{Na}}$, $E_{\mathrm{K}}$, and $E_{\mathrm{Ca}}$, respectively.

## Finding the Nernst potential via Gibbs energy

An even faster way of finding an expression for the Nernst potential is to use the thermodynamics principle that there is zero change in Gibbs free energy for a process at equilibrium ($\Delta G = 0$). Moving an ion across the membrane should incur zero net energy cost if the cell sits exactly at $V = E_{\mathrm{X}}$.

The Gibbs energy for a particle inside the cell is:

```{math}
:label: eq:gibbs_inside
G_{\mathrm{i}} = G^0 + R T \ln [\mathrm{X}]_{\mathrm{i}} + z F V_{\mathrm{i}}
```

While on the outside it is:

```{math}
:label: eq:gibbs_outside
G_{\mathrm{e}} = G^0 + R T \ln [\mathrm{X}]_{\mathrm{e}} + z F V_{\mathrm{e}}
```

When we compute the difference, the standard free energy ($G^0$) cancels out, giving the net change:

```{math}
:label: eq:gibbs_delta
\Delta G = G_{\mathrm{i}} - G_{\mathrm{e}} = R T \ln \frac{[\mathrm{X}]_{\mathrm{i}}}{[\mathrm{X}]_{\mathrm{e}}} + z F (V_{\mathrm{i}} - V_{\mathrm{e}})
```

Setting $\Delta G = 0$ for equilibrium and solving for $V = V_{\mathrm{i}} - V_{\mathrm{e}}$ yields:

```{math}
:label: eq:gibbs_nernst
E_{\mathrm{X}} = \frac{R T}{z F} \ln \frac{[\mathrm{X}]_{\mathrm{e}}}{[\mathrm{X}]_{\mathrm{i}}}
```

While these two derivations look wildly different—one based on flux equations and one on pure thermodynamics—they both rely on the exact same underlying physical truths.

## Typical Nernst potentials for cardiomyocytes

The following table provides typical physiological concentrations for mammalian cardiomyocytes and the resulting Nernst potentials for the three most important ions in cardiac electrophysiology.

| Ion                 | Intracellular | Extracellular | Nernst Potential |
| ------------------- | :-----------: | ------------: | ---------------: |
| Sodium (Na$^+$)     |     8 mM      |        110 mM |           +70 mV |
| Potassium (K$^+$)   |    100 mM     |          4 mM |           -86 mV |
| Calcium (Ca$^{2+}$) |    0.2 µM     |          1 mM |          +114 mV |
