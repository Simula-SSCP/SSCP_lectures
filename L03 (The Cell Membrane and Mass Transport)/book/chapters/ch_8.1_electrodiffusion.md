# Electrodiffusion with the Nernst-Planck equation

If there are open ion channels in the membrane, ions are free to move either into or out of the cell. At the microscopic scale, the motion of individual ions is random and chaotic. However, it is the _net_ macroscopic motion of ions that we want to model.

In the previous physical chemistry session, we covered _diffusive flux_. From Fick's law, we know that the pure diffusive flux of a molecular species $k$ is given by:

```{math}
:label: eq:ficks_flux
J_{k, \mathrm{diff}} = -D_k \nabla c_k
```

The diffusive flux is directly proportional to the gradient of the concentration. If we consider the inside and outside of the cell as _well-mixed_ compartments, then this gradient simply represents the difference in ion concentrations between the inside and outside of the cell. For instance, there is usually far less sodium (Na$^+$) inside the cell than outside it. If sodium channels open, Fick's law dictates that sodium will tend to rush _into_ the cell due to diffusion.

However, diffusion is not the whole story here. Because we are talking about the transport of electrically charged particles, these particles will also be heavily affected by any electrical potentials they encounter. We therefore need to talk about **electrodiffusion**, which is the diffusion of electric charge.

## Electric drift of ions

When subjected to an electric field, charged particles will begin to move, leading to a flux of matter. This flux will be proportional to the strength of the electric field and to the charge of the particle itself. We define this drift flux as:

```{math}
:label: eq:drift_flux
J_{k, \mathrm{drift}} = \frac{D_k z_k F}{R T} c_k \vec{E}
```

Here, $D_k$ is the diffusivity, $z_k$ is the _valency_ of the species (its integer electric charge, e.g., +1 for Na$^+$, +2 for Ca$^{2+}$), and $F$, $R$, and $T$ are the Faraday constant, the ideal gas constant, and the temperature, respectively. Finally, we have the electric field $\vec{E}$.

Any electric field can be mathematically described as the negative gradient of an electric potential ($\vec{E} = -\nabla V$). Because it is the membrane _potential_ ($V$) that we are most interested in modeling, we rewrite the drift equation as:

```{math}
:label: eq:drift_flux_potential
J_{k, \mathrm{drift}} = -\frac{D_k z_k F}{R T} c_k \nabla V
```

This equation tells us that if there is a potential $V$ across the membrane, there will be a flux of charged ions through the channels driven strictly by electrical forces.

## The Nernst-Planck equation

The _net_ flux of ions through an ion channel will be dictated by the combination of diffusion and electrical drift. The total flux can therefore be written out as the sum of these two components:

```{math}
:label: eq:nernst_planck
J_k = J_{k, \mathrm{diff}} + J_{k, \mathrm{drift}} = -D_k\left(\nabla c_k + \frac{z_k F}{R T} c_k \nabla V\right)
```

This powerful formula is known as the **Nernst-Planck equation**, and it describes the net flux of ions through an ion channel due to electrodiffusion. The two terms correspond to diffusion (driven by concentration differences) and drift (driven by potential differences).

Just like Fick's law, the Nernst-Planck flux holds for each species independently, which is why we write it using the sub-index $k$. Note, however, that the potential $V$ is actually generated by the combination of _all_ charged ion concentrations collectively. Therefore, unlike pure diffusion, we cannot solve the Nernst-Planck equation for each species in complete isolation in a dynamic system.

Solving the full spatial Nernst-Planck equation to track the motion of electrically charged ions is, in the general case, extremely difficult. Luckily for us, we can use a few brilliant simplifying assumptions to find the exact equilibrium state of the membrane without solving the full dynamic PDE.
