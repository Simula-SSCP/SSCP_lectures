# From telegraph lines to neurons

The mathematical foundations that shape our modern understanding of biological signal propagation actually emerged from 19th-century telecommunications.

## Early diffusion models (1850s)

During the 1850s, the first attempts were being made to lay a transatlantic telegraph cable. Engineers quickly realized that electrical signals degraded significantly over long distances. **William Thomson** (later known as Lord Kelvin) developed the first mathematical model to understand this signal behaviour in submarine cables.

He modelled the signal propagation as a pure diffusion process, applying mathematical techniques borrowed directly from heat transfer theory. While groundbreaking, his model could not fully explain the observed delays and signal degradation, because it was missing a key physical phenomenon.

## Heaviside's contribution (Late 1870s)

A few decades later, **Oliver Heaviside** advanced transmission line theory by recognizing that signals do not merely diffuse; they also travel as _waves_. By combining diffusion and electromagnetic wave propagation, he created a much more complete model of signal transmission.

| | Kelvin's approach | Heaviside's approach |
|---|---|---|
| Physical model | Diffusion only | Diffusion + waves |
| Mathematical analogy | Heat equation | Electromagnetic wave theory |
| Predictive accuracy | Limited for long cables | Substantially improved |

He formulated the **Telegrapher's Equations**, a set of partial differential equations describing the spatial and temporal evolution of voltage ($V$) and current ($I$) along a transmission line:

```{math}
:label: eq:telegraphers_voltage
\frac{\partial V}{\partial x} = -R \cdot I - L \frac{\partial I}{\partial t}
```

```{math}
:label: eq:telegraphers_current
\frac{\partial I}{\partial x} = -G \cdot V - C \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}
```

Here $R$, $L$, $C$, and $G$ are the resistance, inductance, capacitance, and conductance _per unit length_ of the transmission line:

| Parameter | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| $R$ | Resistance per unit length |
| $L$ | Inductance per unit length |
| $C$ | Capacitance per unit length |
| $G$ | Conductance per unit length |

These four parameters fully characterize the electrical properties of the cable. By combining the two equations, one can derive a single wave equation for the voltage:

```{math}
:label: eq:wave_equation
\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2} = LC \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial t^2} + (RC + LG) \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + RG \cdot V
```

Heaviside's work provided the theoretical foundation for radio frequency conductors, power lines, and modern telecommunications (including fibre optics and 5G networks). Remarkably, this exact same mathematical framework applies perfectly to biological cells.

## Application to neurobiology

To understand how this applies to biology, we must look at the structure of a neuron. Neurons generally consist of three distinct parts:

1. **Dendrites:** The "input stage" of the neuron that receives signals from other cells.
2. **Soma:** The main cell body containing the nucleus and normal cellular functions.
3. **Axon:** The "output" cable of the neuron, which carries the electrical signal over long distances to synapse with neighbouring cells.

```{figure} ../../figs/Blausen_0657_MultipolarNeuron.png

```

In terms of signal conduction, we divide these structures into two categories:

- **Passive conduction:** Dendrites are typically non-excitable. They behave like passive, leaky telegraph cables where the signal simply diffuses and decays over distance.
- **Active conduction:** Axons are excitable. They contain voltage-gated ion channels (like the Hodgkin-Huxley channels we modelled previously) that actively regenerate the electrical signal as it travels, allowing it to propagate indefinitely without decaying.

Electrically, each small segment of the membrane can be modelled as the same RC circuit we encountered in earlier modules: a capacitor (the lipid bilayer) in parallel with resistors (the ion channels).

Whether the conduction is passive or active, the underlying physics of how the current moves down the physical length of the cell is governed by the cable equation.

## Why is the cable equation important?

The cable equation is not merely an academic exercise — it is the fundamental mathematical tool for modelling electrical conduction in excitable tissue. Its applications include:

- **Neuronal modelling:** Understanding how signals propagate along axons and how they attenuate in dendritic trees. The cable equation determines whether a synaptic input is strong enough to reach the soma and trigger an action potential.
- **The cardiac Purkinje network:** The heart's fast-conducting Purkinje fibres behave as one-dimensional cables that rapidly distribute the electrical activation signal throughout the ventricles.
- **Whole-organ cardiac models:** The 1D cable equation generalises to the 3D **bidomain** and **monodomain** equations, which are used to simulate electrical wave propagation across the entire heart. These models are essential tools in modern computational cardiology, underpinning research into arrhythmias, defibrillation, and cardiac resynchronisation therapy.

In this module, we will work exclusively with the 1D cable equation. However, the numerical methods and physical intuition developed here carry over directly to higher-dimensional tissue-level models.
