# Scaling the cable equation

Before we solve the cable equation, it is mathematically useful to scale the variables to reduce the number of floating parameters and make the equation universally applicable.

Let us define a baseline membrane resistance $R_{\mathrm{m}}$:

```{math}
:label: eq:membrane_resistance
\frac{1}{R_{\mathrm{m}}} = \frac{\Delta I_{\mathrm{ion}}}{\Delta V}(V_0)
```

where $V_0$ is the resting potential. If we assume the axial resistances $r_i$ and $r_e$ are constant, we can multiply the entire cable equation by $R_{\mathrm{m}}$ to get:

```{math}
:label: eq:cable_scaled_step1
C_{\mathrm{m}} R_{\mathrm{m}} \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + R_{\mathrm{m}} I_{\mathrm{ion}} = \frac{R_{\mathrm{m}}}{p(r_i+r_e)} \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2}
```

We can group these physical constants into two important biological parameters:

- **The time constant** ($\tau_{\mathrm{m}}$): Defined as $\tau_{\mathrm{m}} = C_{\mathrm{m}} R_{\mathrm{m}}$. This dictates how fast the membrane voltage can change in time.
- **The space constant** ($\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}$): Defined as $\lambda_{\mathrm{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{R_{\mathrm{m}}}{p(r_i+r_e)}}$. This dictates how far a signal will physically travel down the cable before decaying.

By also defining the ionic current term as $f = -R_{\mathrm{m}} I_{\mathrm{ion}}$, we can rewrite the equation as:

```{math}
:label: eq:cable_scaled_step2
\tau_{\mathrm{m}} \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} - f = \lambda_{\mathrm{m}}^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2}
```

Finally, we introduce strictly dimensionless variables for time ($T = t/\tau_{\mathrm{m}}$) and space ($X = x/\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}$). This gives us the clean, dimensionless cable equation:

```{math}
:label: eq:cable_dimensionless
\frac{\partial V}{\partial T} = f + \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial X^2}
```

A solution $\hat{V}(T,X)$ of equation {eq}`eq:cable_dimensionless` implies that $V(t,x) = \hat{V}(t/\tau_{\mathrm{m}},\, x/\lambda_{\mathrm{m}})$ will satisfy the unscaled cable equation. The equation has units of voltage, but can be fully non-dimensionalized by introducing $V = V_0 + \bar{V}\tilde{V}$, where $V_0$ is the resting membrane voltage, $\bar{V}$ is a characteristic voltage, and $\tilde{V}$ is a dimensionless variable.

## The reaction term ($f$)

The form of the reaction term $f$ depends entirely on the specific biology of the cell we want to study:

- **For an excitable axon:** $I_{\mathrm{ion}}$ would be the complex, nonlinear Hodgkin-Huxley equations we derived in the previous module.
- **For a passive dendrite:** We use a simple linear resistance model ($I_{\mathrm{ion}} = \frac{V-V_0}{R_{\mathrm{m}}}$). In scaled form, this simply becomes $f = -V$, yielding the passive linear cable equation:

```{math}
:label: eq:cable_passive
\frac{\partial V}{\partial T} = \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial X^2} - V
```

## Passive conduction and the space constant

Consider a long, passive dendrite governed by the linear equation above. If we voltage-clamp the left end of the cable ($X = 0$) to a constant voltage $V_c$, the system will eventually reach a steady state where the voltage is no longer changing in time ($\frac{\partial V}{\partial T} = 0$).

The equation simplifies to:

```{math}
:label: eq:steady_state_passive
\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial X^2} = V
```

The mathematical solution to this simple spatial differential equation is an exponential decay:

```{math}
:label: eq:passive_decay
V(X) = V_c e^{-X} = V_c e^{-x/\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}}
```

This tells us exactly how a signal decays along a passive cable. At a distance of exactly one space constant ($x = \lambda_{\mathrm{m}}$), the voltage will have decayed to $e^{-1}$, or roughly 37% of its original strength. At a distance of ten space constants, the signal is almost completely erased.

This is exactly why large animals _must_ have active, regenerating action potentials (axons) to send signals over long distances, rather than relying purely on passive diffusion.

## Boundary and initial conditions

To solve the cable equation, we need to specify both initial and boundary conditions.

**Initial conditions** describe the state of the cable at $t = 0$. Common choices include:

- Entire cable at rest: $V(X, 0) = 0$
- Partly activated (piecewise constant): $V(X, 0) = V_0$ for $x < x_0$ and $V(X, 0) = 0$ for $x > x_0$

**Boundary conditions** describe what happens at the ends of the cable. The two standard types are:

- **Dirichlet (voltage clamp):** The voltage at the boundary is held at a fixed value, $V(x_b, T) = V_b$. This models, for example, a patch clamp electrode holding one end of the cable at a known potential.
- **Neumann (current injection):** The spatial derivative of the voltage is specified at the boundary: $\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\big|_{x_b} = -r_i I$. This models the injection of a known current at the boundary. When no current is injected ($I = 0$), the condition $\frac{\partial V}{\partial x} = 0$ is a "sealed end" or no-flux condition.

The Neumann condition follows from the Ohmic relation $\frac{\partial V_i}{\partial x} = -r_i I_i$. If the extracellular resistance is negligible ($r_e \approx 0$), then $V \approx V_i$ and the condition simplifies directly.
