# The Rice (2008) contraction model

The Rice model is widely used in modern computational cardiac mechanics. The complete model features eight state variables and can be conceptually divided into three coupled sub-systems:

1. Calcium binding to Troponin C.
2. Crossbridge (XB) cycling.
3. Force development and sarcomere length changes.

## 1. Calcium binding to Troponin C

The binding of calcium to Troponin C (TnC) is the primary driver for the rest of the system.

Just like in the Razumova model, there is a powerful cooperative feedback mechanism: it is physically easier for calcium to bind to Troponin C if there is already a strongly bound crossbridge sitting nearby! The Rice model handles this by introducing two separate states for bound calcium:

- $\mathrm{TRPN}_{\mathrm{CaL}}$: Calcium bound to _low-affinity_ sites (no nearby crossbridges).
- $\mathrm{TRPN}_{\mathrm{CaH}}$: Calcium bound to _high-affinity_ sites (crossbridges are present).

These two states dictate the transition of the regulatory unit from the "non-permissive" state (closed) to the "permissive" state (open, ready for myosin to bind).

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{TRPN}_{\mathrm{CaL}}}{\mathrm{d}t} = k_{\mathrm{onT}} [\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{i}} (1 - \mathrm{TRPN}_{\mathrm{CaL}}) - k_{\mathrm{offLT}} \mathrm{TRPN}_{\mathrm{CaL}}
$$

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{TRPN}_{\mathrm{CaH}}}{\mathrm{d}t} = k_{\mathrm{onT}} [\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{i}} (1 - \mathrm{TRPN}_{\mathrm{CaH}}) - k_{\mathrm{offHT}} \mathrm{TRPN}_{\mathrm{CaH}}
$$

## 2. Crossbridge cycling states

Once the regulatory unit is in a permissive state, the myosin head can attach and cycle. The model tracks four crossbridge states:

- $N$: Non-permissive state (myosin blocked).
- $P$: Permissive state (myosin allowed to bind, but currently detached).
- $\mathrm{XB}_{\mathrm{PreR}}$: Attached crossbridge, pre-rotation (has not undergone the powerstroke).
- $\mathrm{XB}_{\mathrm{PostR}}$: Attached crossbridge, post-rotation (force-generating).

The transition from $P$ to $\mathrm{XB}_{\mathrm{PreR}}$ represents attachment, and the transition from $\mathrm{XB}_{\mathrm{PreR}}$ to $\mathrm{XB}_{\mathrm{PostR}}$ represents the physical powerstroke.

The transitions between these states lead to the following set of coupled ODEs:

$$
\begin{align*}
\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}t} &= k_{\mathrm{pn}} P - k_{\mathrm{npT}} N \\
\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t} &= k_{\mathrm{npT}} N - k_{\mathrm{pn}} P - f_{\mathrm{appT}} P + g_{\mathrm{appT}} \mathrm{XB}_{\mathrm{PreR}} + g_{\mathrm{xbT}} \mathrm{XB}_{\mathrm{PostR}} \\
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{XB}_{\mathrm{PreR}}}{\mathrm{d}t} &= f_{\mathrm{appT}} P + h_{\mathrm{bT}} \mathrm{XB}_{\mathrm{PostR}} - (g_{\mathrm{appT}} + h_{\mathrm{fT}}) \mathrm{XB}_{\mathrm{PreR}} \\
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{XB}_{\mathrm{PostR}}}{\mathrm{d}t} &= h_{\mathrm{fT}} \mathrm{XB}_{\mathrm{PreR}} - (h_{\mathrm{bT}} + g_{\mathrm{xbT}}) \mathrm{XB}_{\mathrm{PostR}}
\end{align*}
$$

Note that the transition rates $k_{\mathrm{npT}}$ between non-permissive and permissive states depend strongly on $\mathrm{TRPN}_{\mathrm{CaH}}$ and $\mathrm{TRPN}_{\mathrm{CaL}}$, which keeps the RUs locked in the non-permissive state at low calcium and allows the transition when bound calcium increases.

## 3. Force development and distortion

To calculate the actual macroscopic force produced by the muscle, the Rice model treats every attached crossbridge as a microscopic linear spring. Following standard Hooke's Law, the force produced by a single crossbridge is proportional to its physical distortion ($x$):

$$
F_{\mathrm{XB}} = k_{\mathrm{XB}} x
$$

For the entire population of crossbridges, the total macroscopic force is proportional to the number of crossbridges in the strongly bound states ($\mathrm{XB}_{\mathrm{PreR}}$ and $\mathrm{XB}_{\mathrm{PostR}}$), multiplied by their respective _average_ distortions:

$$
F \propto k_{\mathrm{XB}} \left( \mathrm{XB}_{\mathrm{PreR}} \cdot x_{\mathrm{PreR}} + \mathrm{XB}_{\mathrm{PostR}} \cdot x_{\mathrm{PostR}} \right)
$$

The average distortion variables ($x_{\mathrm{PreR}}$ and $x_{\mathrm{PostR}}$) are fully dynamic. Their changes come from two sources:

1. **Sarcomere length changes:** Lengthening or shortening the muscle changes the distortion of all strongly bound XBs (stretching or releasing them). This is incorporated by making XB distortions depend on $\mathrm{dSL}/\mathrm{d}t$.
2. **The powerstroke:** As a crossbridge transitions from pre- to post-rotation, this induces a distortion $x_0$.

Combining these effects yields the distortion ODEs:

$$
\begin{align*}
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{\mathrm{PreR}}}{\mathrm{d}t} &\sim \frac{\mathrm{dSL}}{\mathrm{d}t} + \left(-f_{\mathrm{appT}} x_{\mathrm{PreR}} + h_{\mathrm{bT}} (x_{\mathrm{PostR}} - (x_0 + x_{\mathrm{PreR}}))\right) \\
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{\mathrm{PostR}}}{\mathrm{d}t} &\sim \frac{\mathrm{dSL}}{\mathrm{d}t} + h_{\mathrm{fT}} (x_{\mathrm{PreR}} + x_0 - x_{\mathrm{PostR}})
\end{align*}
$$

## 4. Force-Velocity relations

A fundamental characteristic of both cardiac and skeletal muscle is the **hyperbolic** relationship between force and shortening velocity — first described by Huxley. The F-V relation can typically be described by a modified Hill curve:

$$
v = \frac{a \, v_{\max} - b \, F}{F + a}
$$

where $v$ is shortening velocity, $F$ is the developed force (equal to the applied load), $v_{\max}$ is the maximum shortening velocity (at zero load), and $a$, $b$ are fitted parameters.

To determine this relation computationally, the protocol is:
1. Hold the model at a fixed sarcomere length (e.g. SL = 2.2 µm) with high constant calcium until maximum activation.
2. Release the muscle, allowing it to contract against a constant load. Record the maximum shortening velocity.
3. Repeat for a sequence of load values.

The Rice model reproduces this hyperbolic F-V relation, confirming its physical consistency with the Huxley framework.

## 5. Model limitations

While the model components described above are based on sound physical principles, the published model also contains a number of **phenomenological** components that lack an intuitive physical foundation. These were included to reproduce key features of experimental data without making the model overly complex.

One notable example is the inclusion of *two separate descriptions* of calcium binding — referred to as **regulatory** binding (which drives contraction, as described above) and **apparent** binding (which describes the effect seen by the electrophysiology model in terms of calcium buffering). These two formulations describe the same physical process, but serve different roles in the coupled electro-mechanics framework.

While this duplication is obviously sub-optimal, it was deliberately introduced to avoid too-strong feedback from the mechanics part to the electrophysiology, and to keep the model at a tractable level of complexity. We refer to the original publication for full details on these and other approximations.

By integrating this system alongside a simulated calcium transient, the Rice model successfully bridges the gap between the chemical cloud of calcium and the physical beating of the heart!
