# The mathematics of sliding filaments

The Huxley (1957) model operates on several foundational assumptions regarding the crossbridge (XB) cycle:

1. **No Calcium Activation:** It ignores the complex role of calcium and the thin filament regulatory proteins, assuming the contractile machinery is simply fully active.
2. **The Plateau Region:** It assumes the muscle is operating at an optimal length where the overlap between thick and thin filaments is constant.
3. **Constant Velocity:** It assumes the filaments are sliding past each other at a constant macroscopic velocity, $v$.
4. **Independent Motors:** It assumes that the interaction between one myosin head and an actin site is completely independent of all other neighboring crossbridges.

## The spatial probability distribution

The most important variable in the Huxley model is $x$, the physical distance a myosin head is displaced from its resting equilibrium position (O).

Because there are millions of crossbridges in a single cardiomyocyte, the model assumes that the available actin binding sites are evenly and constantly distributed across a distance $l$ (where $l$ is the physical distance between two actin sites).

Let $n(x,t)$ be the probability that a crossbridge is physically attached to an actin site, given that the site is currently at a displacement $x$ from equilibrium.

At steady state, the overall probability distribution is not changing in time:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}n(x,t)}{\mathrm{d}t} = 0
$$

However, because the filaments are sliding, $x$ itself is a function of time. Applying the chain rule gives us:

$$
\frac{\partial n}{\partial x}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} + \frac{\partial n}{\partial t} = 0
$$

Because the velocity of the sliding filament is defined as $v = -\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}$ (the distance $x$ shrinks as the filament slides forward), we can substitute and rearrange:

$$
-v\frac{\partial n}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial n}{\partial t}
$$

## The rates of attachment and detachment

The partial derivative $\frac{\partial n}{\partial t}$ simply represents the physical rate at which myosin heads attach to actin, minus the rate at which they detach. We can define this using standard mass action:

$$
\frac{\partial n}{\partial t} = f(x)[1 - n(x,t)] - g(x)n(x,t)
$$

Here, $f(x)$ is the rate of attachment, and $g(x)$ is the rate of detachment. Notice that both rates are strictly dependent on the physical displacement $x$.

Because we are at steady state ($\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}t} = 0$), the time dependence drops out, and we can combine our two equations:

$$
-v\frac{\mathrm{d}n(x)}{\mathrm{d}x} = f(x)[1 - n(x)] - g(x)n(x)
$$

## Defining the rate functions

Huxley defined the rates $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ as piecewise functions based on three distinct spatial regions:

$$
\begin{cases}
x < 0: & f(x) = 0, \quad g(x) = g_2 \\
0 < x < h: & f(x) = \frac{f_1 x}{h}, \quad g(x) = \frac{g_1 x}{h} \\
x > h: & f(x) = 0, \quad g(x) = \frac{g_1 x}{h}
\end{cases}
$$

This mathematical choice was brilliantly simple but physically profound:

- **Region 2 ($0 < x < h$):** The myosin head is stretched forward. It attaches rapidly ($f_1$) and pulls.
- **Region 1 ($x < 0$):** The myosin head has been dragged backward past its equilibrium point. It is now actively resisting the sliding motion! Therefore, it must detach incredibly rapidly ($g_2$) so it doesn't act as a brake.
- **Region 3 ($x > h$):** The myosin head is stretched too far forward to physically reach the actin binding site, so attachment is impossible ($f = 0$).

## The analytical solution

Because the ODE is piecewise linear in each region, it can be solved analytically by imposing **continuity constraints** at the boundaries ($n(0^-)=n(0^+)$ and $n(h^-)=n(h^+)$) and the physical requirement that $n(x)=0$ for $x > h$.

:::{admonition} Full derivation
:class: seealso

The complete step-by-step derivation---separating variables, evaluating integration constants, and simplifying---is presented in {ref}`sec_huxley_derivation`.
:::

Introducing the grouped parameter $\phi = \frac{h}{S}(f_1 + g_1)$ (where $S$ is the sarcomere length) and the normalised velocity $V_{\mathrm{norm}} = \frac{2v}{S}$, the final piecewise solutions are:

$$
\begin{cases}
x < 0: & n(x) = \dfrac{f_1}{f_1+g_1}\left(1-e^{-\phi/V_{\mathrm{norm}}}\right) e^{2g_2 x/(S V_{\mathrm{norm}})} \\[6pt]
0 \le x \le h: & n(x) = \dfrac{f_1}{f_1+g_1}\left(1-e^{\left(\frac{x^2}{h^2}-1\right)\frac{\phi}{V_{\mathrm{norm}}}}\right) \\[6pt]
x > h: & n(x) = 0
\end{cases}
$$

## Macroscopic force: the Force-Velocity relation

Huxley modelled each attached crossbridge as a **linear spring** with stiffness $k$:

$$
F_{\mathrm{XB}} = k x
$$

The expected value of the total tension produced by the entire crossbridge population is obtained by integrating over the uniform spatial distribution:

$$
\langle T \rangle = \int_{-l/2}^{l/2} \frac{k x}{l}\, n(x)\, \mathrm{d}x
$$

Substituting the piecewise $n(x)$ and carrying out the integration yields the elegant closed-form result:

$$
\langle T \rangle = \frac{f_1}{f_1+g_1}\frac{k h^2}{2l}\left(1-\frac{V_{\mathrm{norm}}}{\phi}\left(1-e^{-\phi/V_{\mathrm{norm}}}\right)\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{f_1+g_1}{g_2}\right)^2\frac{V_{\mathrm{norm}}}{\phi}\right)
$$

At zero velocity ($V_{\mathrm{norm}}=0$), the muscle produces its maximum isometric tension:

$$
T_{\max} = \frac{f_1}{f_1+g_1}\frac{k h^2}{2l}
$$

Huxley determined that the best-fit parameter ratios to match experimental data are:

$$
\frac{g_1}{f_1+g_1} = \frac{3}{16}, \qquad \frac{g_2}{f_1+g_1} = 3.919
$$

By making a few brilliant assumptions about the microscopic probabilities of a single myosin head attaching to actin, Huxley's 1957 model mathematically derives the exact hyperbolic macroscopic Force-Velocity curve observed in whole muscle tissue.
