# Assembling the full cell model

To simulate a whole cell, we return to the basic RC circuit model of the membrane. The total current crossing the membrane dictates how the membrane potential changes over time. According to Kirchhoff's current law, the capacitive current must perfectly balance the sum of all ionic currents and any externally applied stimulus current:

$$
C_{\mathrm{m}} \frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t} = - (I_{\mathrm{Na}} + I_{\mathrm{K}} + I_{\mathrm{L}} + I_{\mathrm{app}})
$$

To calculate the right-hand side of this equation, we simply substitute the Ohmic definitions for each specific current.

## The Hodgkin-Huxley Currents

The classic Hodgkin-Huxley model of the squid giant axon contains three distinct ionic currents:

1. **The Sodium Current ($I_{\mathrm{Na}}$):** This channel has both an activation gate ($m$) and an inactivation gate ($h$). Because it has three identical activation voltage sensors, the $m$ variable is cubed.

   $$
   I_{\mathrm{Na}} = \bar{g}_{\mathrm{Na}} m^3 h (V - E_{\mathrm{Na}})
   $$

2. **The Potassium Current ($I_{\mathrm{K}}$):** This is a delayed rectifier channel. It has an activation gate ($n$) but no inactivation gate. It has four identical voltage sensors, so the $n$ variable is raised to the fourth power.

   $$
   I_{\mathrm{K}} = \bar{g}_{\mathrm{K}} n^4 (V - E_{\mathrm{K}})
   $$

3. **The Leak Current ($I_{\mathrm{L}}$):** This represents the constant background leak of ions (primarily chloride and potassium) that helps maintain the resting membrane potential. It is not voltage-gated, so its open probability is simply 1.

   $$
   I_{\mathrm{L}} = \bar{g}_{\mathrm{L}} (V - E_{\mathrm{L}})
   $$

## The "Tug-of-War" of the Action Potential

When we put these equations together, the generation of an action potential becomes a beautifully choreographed thermodynamic tug-of-war.

When a stimulus current ($I_{\mathrm{app}}$) slightly depolarizes the cell, the fast-acting $m$ gates of the sodium channels snap open. Sodium rushes into the cell, trying to pull the membrane potential toward the highly positive $E_{\mathrm{Na}}$ (+50 mV). This causes a runaway positive feedback loop (the upstroke of the action potential).

However, the cell does not stay depolarized forever, thanks to two delayed mechanisms. First, the slower $h$ gates of the sodium channels begin to shut, cutting off the inward sodium current (inactivation). Second, the slow $n$ gates of the potassium channels finally begin to open, allowing potassium to rush out of the cell, forcefully dragging the membrane potential back down toward $E_{\mathrm{K}}$ (-80 mV).
