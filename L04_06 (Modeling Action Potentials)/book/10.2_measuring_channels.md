# Measuring ion channels

To build accurate mathematical models of ion channels, we need highly precise data. The history of electrophysiology is defined by a series of brilliant experimental setups designed to coax these tiny electrical signals out of living cells.

## A brief history of experimental setups

### Curtis and Cole (1930s)

During the 1920s and 1930s, Howard Curtis and Kenneth Cole utilized a Wheatstone bridge arrangement to measure the resistance of the cell membrane. Working with the **squid giant axon**—a massive nerve fiber up to 1 mm in diameter—they proved that the cell membrane behaves as a parallel RC circuit, and that it undergoes a massive, brief drop in resistance during an action potential.

### Hodgkin and Huxley (1950s)

Building on Cole's work, Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley developed the **two-electrode voltage clamp** method. By inserting one wire down the length of the squid giant axon to inject current, and another to measure voltage, they were able to artificially "clamp" the membrane potential at specific voltages. This allowed them to meticulously isolate and measure the underlying $\mathrm{Na}^+$ and $\mathrm{K}^+$ currents, resulting in the famous Hodgkin-Huxley model. They were awarded the Nobel Prize in 1963.

### Sakmann and Neher (1980s)

For 30 years after Hodgkin and Huxley, the molecular basis of the currents was debated. Most suspected discrete ion channels, but the currents of single proteins ($\sim$1 pA) were too small to observe due to thermal noise. In 1983, Bert Sakmann and Erwin Neher successfully created a tight glass-to-membrane seal using a microscopic pipette. The **patch-clamp technique** finally allowed researchers to record the distinct opening and closing of a _single_ ion channel.

## Macroscopic vs. Single-Channel Currents

While the patch-clamp technique lets us watch a single channel pop open and shut stochastically, whole-cell models (like neurons and cardiomyocytes) contain tens of thousands of channels.

When observing the entire cell, the current appears incredibly smooth and continuous. This **macroscopic current** is simply the sum of thousands of single-channel events. We can mathematically define the macroscopic current ($I$) as:

$$
I = N \cdot i \cdot P_o
$$

Where $N$ is the total number of channels, $i$ is the single-channel (unitary) current, and $P_o$ is the **open probability** of the channels (a number between 0 and 1).

In macroscopic modeling, we essentially ignore the discrete nature of single channels and instead focus entirely on modeling the smooth function $P_o$ as it evolves over time and voltage.
