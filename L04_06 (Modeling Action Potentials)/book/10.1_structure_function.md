# Structure-function relationships in ion channel gating

In general, our computational models of ion channel function are abstract; they are built to represent _measurable function_ rather than tracking the exact 3D coordinates of every atom in the protein. However, to build models that make biophysical sense, we must ground them in structural reality.

We know that ion channels contain a **selectivity filter**—a narrow physical pore that strips the hydration shell off ions, allowing only specific ions (like $\mathrm{Na}^+$ or $\mathrm{K}^+$) to pass. But what structures dictate when that pore is open or closed?

## The Voltage Sensor Domain (VSD)

In the figure above, you can see the core ($\alpha$) subunits of major voltage-gated channels. These are composed of either one ($\mathrm{K}^+$ channels) or four ($\mathrm{Na}^+$ and $\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$ channels) linked transmembrane domains.

Importantly for our modeling, each of these transmembrane domains contains a **voltage sensor domain (VSD)**. A VSD is a small stretch of the protein helix containing several positively charged amino acids (often Arginines). Because it carries a net positive charge, the VSD is highly sensitive to the electrical field across the membrane.

When the cell depolarizes (the inside becomes more positive), electrostatic repulsion forces these positively charged VSDs to shift physically outward. This subtle mechanical shift is transferred to the pore, pulling it open. This movement of charge within the membrane can actually be measured experimentally as a tiny, fleeting current known as a _gating current_.

## The Four Phases of Gating

It is conventional to describe the function of ion channels in terms of four experimentally measurable processes:

1. **Activation:** Channel opening in response to a stimulus.
2. **Deactivation:** Channel closure (the direct reversal of activation).
3. **Inactivation:** Channel closure via a completely separate physical mechanism.
4. **Recovery from inactivation:** Return to a state permissive of activation (the reversal of inactivation).

### Activation and Deactivation

Activation is driven by the outward movement of the VSDs. For modeling, an important characteristic is that opening the pore typically requires the transition of _all four_ voltage sensors to the "open" configuration.

Deactivation is simply the reverse process. As the membrane potential returns to a negative resting value, the VSDs are pulled back inward, and the pore snaps shut.

### Inactivation and Recovery

Inactivation is a more varied process than activation. One of the most famous mechanisms is the "ball-and-chain" model (N-type inactivation).

This mechanism was originally established for the family of $\mathrm{K}^+$ channels that carry the A-type current in neurons and the transient outward current ($I_{\mathrm{to}}$) in the heart. An intracellular portion of the protein literally acts like a tethered plug.

An incredibly important characteristic of this type of inactivation is that it **relies upon activation first**. The "ball" can only plug the channel once the pore has physically opened to expose the binding site! This means that the processes of activation and inactivation are intrinsically linked.

Other forms of inactivation exist. C-type inactivation involves structural collapse of the outer pore itself, while $\mathrm{Na}^+$ channel inactivation revolves around a fast-acting linker region between the transmembrane domains. Regardless of the physical mechanism, the computational result is the same: the channel temporarily shuts down, stopping the flow of current even if the cell remains depolarized.
