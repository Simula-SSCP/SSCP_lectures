# The shift to Markov models

Up to this point, we have modeled ion channel gating using the Hodgkin-Huxley (HH) framework, where activation ($m$) and inactivation ($h$) are treated as strictly independent variables. The total open probability is simply their product ($m^3 h$).

This independent formulation is mathematically elegant, computationally cheap, and perfectly adequate for many general-purpose neuroscience and cardiac models. However, it completely breaks down when we need to model modern pharmacological interventions or complex biological interdependencies.

## Why Hodgkin-Huxley fails for pharmacology

Imagine you are a pharmaceutical researcher designing a new drug to treat arrhythmias. Your drug molecules don't just float in the void; they physically enter the channel pore and block it.

Crucially, many drugs exhibit **state-dependent block**. For example, a drug might only be able to enter and block the channel when the activation gate is _open_, but it gets trapped inside when the inactivation gate _closes_.

In a standard HH model, $m$ and $h$ are just abstract mathematical probabilities running in parallel. There is no explicitly defined state where the channel is "activated but not yet inactivated." Because HH models do not track the distinct physical states of the protein, you cannot accurately mathematically bind a drug to just one specific configuration.

## The Markov state solution

To solve this, modern computational electrophysiologists rely on **continuous-time Markov models**.

Instead of treating the gates independently, a Markov model maps out every distinct physical state the entire protein complex can exist in.

- **Closed States (C1, C2, C3...)**
- **Open State (O)**
- **Inactivated States (I_fast, I_slow...)**
- **Drug-Bound States (O_blocked, I_blocked...)**

The model is then defined by a matrix of transition rates between these highly specific states. If a drug only binds to the open state, we simply add a transition path from the **O** state to a new **O_blocked** state, governed by the drug's concentration and binding affinity.

While Markov models are vastly more computationally expensive—often requiring the solver to integrate 10 to 15 differential equations for a _single_ ion current—they are the gold standard for modern drug safety testing and capturing the true biophysical reality of the channel.
