# Experimental protocols for parameterizing models

We now have the mathematical framework for our gating equations. But if you were building a brand new model of a heart cell, how would you figure out the exact values for $m_\infty$ and $\tau_m$ to use in your code?

Electrophysiologists use a series of highly standardized whole-cell voltage clamp protocols to extract this data.

## 1. Steady-State Activation (Measuring $m_\infty$)

This protocol is used to find the target activation curve, $m_\infty(V)$.

The cell is held at a highly negative _holding potential_ where all channels are closed. Then, the voltage is instantaneously stepped up to a specific test potential (e.g., -40 mV). The channels will rapidly open, causing a spike in the macroscopic current. The researcher measures the absolute peak of this current.

The cell is then allowed to rest, and the protocol is repeated for progressively higher test potentials (-30 mV, -20 mV, etc.). By plotting the peak conductance measured at each step, the researcher can trace out the classic S-shaped Boltzmann curve of $m_\infty(V)$.

## 2. Steady-State Inactivation (Measuring $h_\infty$)

If activation measures how many channels _can_ open, inactivation measures how many channels are _available_ to open.

The protocol involves holding the cell at various _conditioning potentials_ for a long period of time to allow the inactivation gate ($h$) to reach its steady-state. Then, a massive test pulse is applied to force all non-inactivated channels to open.

If the conditioning pulse was very negative, the resulting current will be huge (because no channels were inactivated). If the conditioning pulse was positive, the resulting current will be zero (because every channel was inactivated by the conditioning pulse before the test pulse even began!). Plotting these peak currents yields the $h_\infty(V)$ curve.

## 3. Recovery from Inactivation (Measuring $\tau_h$)

The frequency-dependence of the heart is closely linked to how quickly channels recover from their inactivated states back to their closed states, making them available to fire again.

The protocol involves a simple sequence of paired test pulses. The first pulse is huge, designed to violently activate and then inactivate every channel in the cell. The voltage is then dropped back to rest for a specific time delay ($\Delta t$). Finally, a second test pulse is fired.

If $\Delta t$ is very short (1 ms), the second pulse will produce zero current, because the channels haven't had time to recover. By systematically increasing $\Delta t$ and measuring the current from the second pulse, researchers can trace out the exact exponential recovery curve and calculate the time constant $\tau_h$!
