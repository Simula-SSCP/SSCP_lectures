# Measuring ion channels

To build accurate mathematical models of ion channels, we need highly precise experimental data. The history of electrophysiology is defined by a series of brilliant experimental techniques, each designed to coax progressively smaller electrical signals out of living cells. In this section, we will review the key experimental milestones that made computational electrophysiology possible.

## Curtis and Cole: the membrane is a variable resistor

During the 1920s and 1930s, Howard Curtis and Kenneth Cole utilized a Wheatstone bridge arrangement to measure the electrical impedance of the cell membrane. Working with the **squid giant axon**—a massive nerve fiber up to 1 mm in diameter, chosen precisely because its enormous size made it possible to work with—they proved that the cell membrane behaves as a parallel RC circuit. More importantly, they observed that the membrane undergoes a massive, transient drop in resistance during an action potential.

This was a crucial finding. It meant that the action potential was not simply a discharge of the membrane capacitor, but involved a dramatic, temporary increase in the membrane's ionic conductance. Something was opening up in the membrane to let current flow, and then closing again.

## The voltage clamp: isolating ionic currents

The next breakthrough came from Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley in the early 1950s. They developed the **two-electrode voltage clamp** method, which remains one of the most elegant experimental designs in all of physiology.

The key insight is this: recall from our membrane model that the membrane potential changes according to

$$
C_\mathrm{m} \frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\sum_k I_k
$$

In a freely behaving cell, the voltage is constantly changing, which means the ionic currents are also constantly changing, making it very difficult to study any single current in isolation. The voltage clamp solves this problem by using a feedback amplifier to _force_ the membrane potential to stay at a fixed value. If $V$ is held constant, then $\mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}t = 0$, and the current injected by the amplifier to maintain the clamp must be exactly equal to the total ionic current flowing through the membrane. This gives us a direct, clean measurement of the ionic currents at any voltage we choose.

```{figure} ../../fig/HH_Exp.png
---
width: 500px
name: fig_hh_exp
---
The voltage clamp arrangement used by Hodgkin and Huxley in 1952. By inserting one wire down the length of the squid giant axon to inject current, and another to measure voltage, they could artificially "clamp" the membrane at specific voltages and record the resulting ionic currents. *(Adapted from Hille, Ion Channels of Excitable Membranes)*
```

By stepping the voltage to different levels and recording the resulting currents, Hodgkin and Huxley were able to systematically separate the contributions of $\mathrm{Na}^+$ and $\mathrm{K}^+$ channels. They discovered that applying the drug tetrodotoxin (TTX) selectively blocks $\mathrm{Na}^+$ channels, isolating the $\mathrm{K}^+$ current, and vice versa with tetraethylammonium (TEA). From these isolated current traces, they derived the mathematical model that bears their names. They were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1963.

## The patch clamp: single-channel recordings

For 30 years after Hodgkin and Huxley's work, the molecular basis of ionic currents was debated. Most researchers suspected that discrete ion channel proteins existed, but the currents carried by single proteins ($\sim$1 pA) were far too small to observe due to thermal noise.

In 1976, Bert Sakmann and Erwin Neher solved this problem with the **patch-clamp technique**. By pressing a carefully fire-polished glass micropipette against the cell membrane and applying gentle suction, they created an extraordinarily tight seal (a "gigaseal") with a resistance on the order of gigaohms. This seal reduced background noise so dramatically that they could finally resolve the tiny currents flowing through a _single_ ion channel protein.

```{figure} ../../fig/Patch2.png
---
width: 400px
name: fig_patch_clamp_setup
---
Schematic of a patch-clamp recording setup. A glass micropipette is pressed against the cell membrane. The tight seal between the glass and the membrane isolates a small patch of membrane containing one or a few channels.
```

The patch-clamp technique revealed something beautiful: single channels do not open gradually. They flick between discrete conducting and non-conducting states in an all-or-nothing, stochastic fashion. A channel is either fully open, carrying its characteristic unitary current $i$, or fully closed. This binary behavior is the microscopic reality underlying the smooth macroscopic currents that Hodgkin and Huxley measured.

```{figure} ../../fig/Patch.png

**Top:** A patch-clamp recording showing single-channel opening events as brief, square-shaped downward deflections. Each deflection represents a single channel protein flickering between its open and closed conformations. **Bottom:** The amplitude histogram confirms that the channel conducts a fixed unitary current when open.
```

Sakmann and Neher were awarded the Nobel Prize in 1991 for this work, which transformed our understanding of ion channel biology.
