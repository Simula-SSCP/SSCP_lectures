# Structure-function relationships in ion channel gating

We established in the previous module that ions cannot cross the lipid bilayer on their own—the hydrophobic interior of the membrane presents an insurmountable energy barrier for charged particles. Ion channels are the specialized proteins that solve this problem by providing a hydrophilic pore through the membrane. But a channel that is always open would be useless for generating action potentials; what makes ion channels so remarkable is their ability to _switch_ between conducting and non-conducting conformations.

In this section, we will explore the structural features that allow ion channels to sense the membrane potential and respond to it. Our computational models of ion channel function are necessarily abstract—they represent _measurable function_ rather than tracking the exact 3D coordinates of every atom in the protein. However, to build models that make biophysical sense, we must ground them in structural reality.

## Channel architecture

The figure below shows the core structure of two major voltage-gated ion channels. Voltage-gated $\mathrm{K}^+$ channels are composed of four identical subunits, each containing six transmembrane segments (labeled S1–S6). The channel pore is formed by the S5–S6 segments from all four subunits coming together. Voltage-gated $\mathrm{Na}^+$ and $\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$ channels have a similar architecture, but instead of four separate subunits, their four domains are fused into a single large protein.

At the center of the pore sits the **selectivity filter**—a narrow constriction lined with precisely positioned oxygen atoms that strips the hydration shell off incoming ions. This filter is what makes a potassium channel select for $\mathrm{K}^+$ over $\mathrm{Na}^+$ with extraordinary specificity (a ratio of about 1000:1), despite $\mathrm{Na}^+$ being a smaller ion. The key insight is that the filter's geometry perfectly replaces the water molecules that normally surround $\mathrm{K}^+$, but does not fit $\mathrm{Na}^+$ as well.

## The voltage sensor domain

But what structures dictate _when_ the pore is open or closed? The answer lies in segments S1–S4 of each subunit, which together form the **voltage sensor domain (VSD)**. The S4 segment is particularly important: it contains a series of positively charged amino acids (typically arginines) spaced at regular intervals along the helix.

Because the VSD carries a net positive charge, it is highly sensitive to the electrical field across the membrane. You can think of it as a charged paddle embedded in the membrane. When the cell is at its resting potential (around $-80$ mV), the electric field pulls these positive charges inward. When the cell depolarizes, the electrostatic force reverses direction, pushing the S4 helix outward.

This physical displacement of the S4 helix is mechanically coupled to the pore domain: when the VSDs shift outward, they pull the S6 helices apart, opening the pore. When they return inward, the pore closes. This movement of charge within the membrane can actually be measured experimentally as a tiny, fleeting current known as a _gating current_—direct evidence that the VSDs are physically moving in response to voltage.

## The four phases of gating

It is conventional to describe the function of ion channels in terms of four experimentally measurable processes:

1. **Activation:** Channel opening in response to a depolarizing stimulus.
2. **Deactivation:** Channel closure when the stimulus is removed (the direct reversal of activation).
3. **Inactivation:** Channel closure via a completely separate physical mechanism, even while the depolarizing stimulus persists.
4. **Recovery from inactivation:** Return to a resting state from which the channel can activate again.

### Activation and deactivation

Activation is driven by the outward movement of the VSDs. For modeling, an important characteristic is that opening the pore typically requires the transition of _all four_ voltage sensors to the "permissive" configuration. If each of the four subunits must independently shift before the pore can open, this immediately suggests a highly nonlinear dependence on voltage—a key feature we will formalize mathematically in the next chapter.

Deactivation is simply the reverse process. As the membrane potential returns to a negative resting value, the VSDs are pulled back inward, and the pore snaps shut. Because activation and deactivation use the same physical mechanism (just in opposite directions), they are governed by the same pair of rate constants, $\alpha$ (opening) and $\beta$ (closing).

### Inactivation and recovery

Inactivation is fundamentally different from deactivation. While deactivation reverses the opening process, inactivation involves a _separate_ structural change that blocks current flow through a mechanism distinct from pore closure.

One of the most famous mechanisms is the **ball-and-chain** model (also called N-type inactivation). An intracellular portion of the protein—a short amino acid sequence tethered to the channel—literally acts as a plug. After the pore opens, this "ball" swings inward and physically blocks the entrance from the inside.


This mechanism was originally established for $\mathrm{K}^+$ channels carrying the A-type current in neurons and the transient outward current ($I_{\mathrm{to}}$) in the heart. An incredibly important characteristic is that ball-and-chain inactivation **requires activation first**—the "ball" can only plug the channel once the pore has physically opened to expose the binding site. This means that activation and inactivation are intrinsically linked: the channel must open before it can inactivate.

Other forms of inactivation exist. C-type inactivation involves a slow structural collapse of the outer selectivity filter. $\mathrm{Na}^+$ channel inactivation involves a fast-acting linker region between domains III and IV that folds inward to block the pore. Regardless of the physical mechanism, the computational result is the same: the channel temporarily shuts down, stopping the flow of current even while the cell remains depolarized.

Recovery from inactivation is the process by which the channel returns from the inactivated state to a closed state that is once again available for activation. This process typically requires the membrane to repolarize, allowing the inactivation particle to unbind and the channel to "reset."
