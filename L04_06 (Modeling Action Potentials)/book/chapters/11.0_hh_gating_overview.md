# The Hodgkin-Huxley gating equations

We have now seen the biological reality of ion channels: proteins that physically change shape in response to voltage, flickering stochastically between open and closed states. We also saw how the random behavior of individual channels averages out at the macroscopic level, producing smooth, deterministic currents.

The next step is to turn this biological understanding into mathematics.

In 1952, Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley published a series of papers that remain among the most influential in all of biology. Working with the squid giant axon—chosen because its enormous diameter (up to 1 mm) made it possible to insert electrodes inside—they used the voltage-clamp technique to systematically measure how sodium and potassium conductances change with voltage and time.

From these measurements, they derived a set of ordinary differential equations describing how _gating variables_ evolve over time. These gating variables represent the probability that the molecular gates of an ion channel are in their permissive configuration. Remarkably, the mathematical framework they developed turns out to be a direct application of the law of mass action that we studied in the physical chemistry module.

In this chapter, we will derive the Hodgkin-Huxley gating equations from first principles, explore how voltage dependence arises naturally from the structure of the rate constants, and present the specific rate functions that Hodgkin and Huxley fitted to their experimental data.
