# Voltage-dependent rates

If the transition rates $\alpha$ and $\beta$ in our gating equation were strict constants, the open probability would simply settle to a single fixed value and stay there forever. Every channel would behave identically regardless of the membrane potential.

In reality, however, gating is a profoundly _voltage-dependent_ process. We saw in the previous chapter that the voltage sensor domains carry positive charges that physically move in response to the membrane's electric field. A depolarized membrane pushes the sensors outward, promoting opening; a hyperpolarized membrane pulls them back, promoting closure. The rate at which these transitions occur must therefore depend strongly on the membrane voltage $V$.

## Exponential rate functions

How should $\alpha(V)$ and $\beta(V)$ depend on voltage? The physical basis comes from transition state theory and the Arrhenius equation (which we encountered in the physical chemistry module). The energy barrier that a charged voltage sensor must overcome to move from one conformation to another depends linearly on the electric field, which leads to an _exponential_ dependence of the rate constant on voltage.

A general form for a voltage-dependent rate constant is:

$$
\alpha(V) = A \, \exp\!\left(\frac{V - V_0}{d}\right)
$$

where $V_0$ is the voltage at which the rate equals the pre-exponential factor $A$, and $d$ is a slope factor that controls how steeply the rate changes with voltage. If $d > 0$, the rate increases with depolarization; if $d < 0$, it decreases.

By defining $\alpha$ and $\beta$ as functions of voltage, our ODE for the gating variable $m$ becomes a dynamic, voltage-driven system:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}t} = \alpha(V) (1 - m) - \beta(V) m
$$

The crucial consequence is that every time the voltage changes—whether from an applied stimulus or from the cell's own ionic currents—the rate constants immediately shift to new values, causing the gating variable to relax toward a new target.

## Steady-state and time constant curves

While the $\alpha$/$\beta$ formulation is how the equation is derived from mass action, electrophysiologists vastly prefer to characterize channel behavior using two physically intuitive quantities: the **steady-state open probability** $m_\infty(V)$ and the **time constant** $\tau_m(V)$. As we derived in the previous section:

$$
m_\infty(V) = \frac{\alpha(V)}{\alpha(V) + \beta(V)}, \qquad \tau_m(V) = \frac{1}{\alpha(V) + \beta(V)}
$$

These two curves completely characterize the gating behavior at every voltage. Let us examine their shapes.

### The steady-state and time constant curves

Because $m_\infty(V)$ represents a transition between two distinct conformational states driven by the electric field, its shape follows a thermodynamic **Boltzmann distribution**. It takes the form of an S-shaped (sigmoidal) curve:

$$
m_\infty(V) \approx \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\left(-\frac{V - V_{1/2}}{k}\right)}
$$

where $V_{1/2}$ is the **half-activation voltage** (the voltage at which exactly 50% of channels are open) and $k$ is the **slope factor** (which controls the steepness of the transition). The steepness is directly related to the amount of charge that moves during the gating transition — channels whose voltage sensors carry more charge have steeper curves and switch more sharply between open and closed.
The time constant $\tau_m(V)$ tells us how fast the channel responds to a voltage change. Its shape is typically a bell curve, peaking near $V_{1/2}$ where $\alpha \approx \beta$ and neither rate dominates. At extreme voltages, one rate overwhelms the other and the gate responds quickly.

## Comparing the three HH gates

Different ion channels have very different steady-state and time-constant curves. This diversity is precisely what allows the cell to orchestrate the complex sequence of current changes that produces an action potential. The three gates in the Hodgkin-Huxley model illustrate this beautifully:

- **Sodium activation ($m$):** Very fast ($\tau_m \sim 0.1$ ms). The $m_\infty$ curve is steep and shifted to relatively negative voltages, meaning sodium channels activate quickly and at moderate depolarizations.
- **Sodium inactivation ($h$):** Slower ($\tau_h \sim 1$ ms). The $h_\infty$ curve is _inverted_—high at rest and low during depolarization—reflecting the fact that inactivation _closes_ the channel. Crucially, $h$ is slower than $m$, so the channel activates first and then inactivates.
- **Potassium activation ($n$):** Intermediate speed ($\tau_n \sim 1-5$ ms). The $n_\infty$ curve is shifted rightward compared to $m_\infty$, meaning potassium channels activate at more positive voltages and with a delay relative to sodium.

This hierarchy of speeds and voltage sensitivities—$m$ fastest, then $h$, then $n$—is the fundamental mechanism behind the action potential shape, as we will see when we put everything together in the next chapter.
