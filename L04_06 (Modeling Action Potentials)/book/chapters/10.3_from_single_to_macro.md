# From single channels to macroscopic currents

We have just seen that individual ion channels flicker stochastically between open and closed states. Each opening event is random—unpredictable in its exact timing and duration. Yet when we record from an entire cell, the total ionic current appears smooth, continuous, and perfectly reproducible from trial to trial. How do we reconcile these two observations?

The answer is the **law of large numbers**: when we sum together the random behavior of thousands of independent channels, the fluctuations average out. The result is a smooth, deterministic signal that can be described by ordinary differential equations.

## The macroscopic current

Consider a cell membrane containing $N$ identical ion channels. At any instant, each channel is either open (conducting its unitary current $i$) or closed. The total macroscopic current is simply the sum:

$$
I = N \cdot i \cdot P_o
$$

where $P_o$ is the **open probability**—the fraction of channels that are open at that moment. This is a number between 0 (all channels closed) and 1 (all channels open).

The figure below illustrates this beautifully. The bottom traces show recordings from single channels: each one flickers on and off at random, with no two traces looking alike. But when we sum these individual traces, the result (top) is a smooth, reproducible current that rises rapidly and then decays—a macroscopic current.

## Why this justifies ODEs

This observation has a profound consequence for modeling. If a cell has $N = 1{,}000$ channels of a given type, the statistical fluctuations in $P_o$ will be on the order of $1/\sqrt{N} \approx 3\%$—barely noticeable. For a real cardiac cell with perhaps 300,000 sodium channels, the noise is less than 0.2%. This is why the Hodgkin-Huxley equations—which describe the time evolution of $P_o$ as a smooth, deterministic function—work so well.

In our mathematical models, we will therefore ignore the stochastic reality of single channels entirely and focus on the deterministic evolution of the open probability $P_o(t, V)$. We refer to $P_o$ as the **gating variable**, and the goal of the next chapter is to derive the differential equations that govern its behavior.

There are situations where stochastic channel behavior matters—for instance, in very small cells with few channels, or near the threshold for excitation where a single channel opening can tip the balance. But for the large, well-populated membranes of the squid axon or a typical cardiomyocyte, the deterministic approximation is excellent.
