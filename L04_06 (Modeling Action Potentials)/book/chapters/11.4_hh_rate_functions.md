# The Hodgkin-Huxley rate functions

We have now established the general mathematical framework for voltage-dependent gating. The next step is to look at the _specific_ rate functions that Hodgkin and Huxley fitted to their experimental data from the squid giant axon.

An important point to understand is that these rate functions were **not derived from first principles**. Hodgkin and Huxley measured ionic currents at many different clamp voltages, extracted the time constants and steady-state values at each voltage, and then searched for mathematical functions $\alpha(V)$ and $\beta(V)$ that reproduced their data. The functional forms they chose were empirical fits—guided by physical intuition about exponential voltage dependence, but ultimately chosen because they worked.

## The rate function formulas

The rate functions for the three gating variables in the HH model are:

### Sodium activation ($m$ gate)

$$
\alpha_m(V) = \frac{0.1(V + 40)}{1 - e^{-(V+40)/10}}, \qquad \beta_m(V) = 4 \, e^{-(V+65)/18}
$$

### Sodium inactivation ($h$ gate)

$$
\alpha_h(V) = 0.07 \, e^{-(V+65)/20}, \qquad \beta_h(V) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(V+35)/10}}
$$

### Potassium activation ($n$ gate)

$$
\alpha_n(V) = \frac{0.01(V + 55)}{1 - e^{-(V+55)/10}}, \qquad \beta_n(V) = 0.125 \, e^{-(V+65)/80}
$$

Note that these are written with voltage in millivolts and rates in $\mathrm{ms}^{-1}$, using the modern convention where the resting potential is approximately $-65$ mV. (Hodgkin and Huxley originally used a convention where the resting potential was defined as 0 mV, so the voltage values in these equations are shifted by 65 mV compared to the original 1952 paper.)

## What do these curves look like?

From the rate functions, we can compute the steady-state values and time constants at every voltage:

$$
m_\infty(V) = \frac{\alpha_m}{\alpha_m + \beta_m}, \qquad \tau_m(V) = \frac{1}{\alpha_m + \beta_m}
$$

and similarly for $h_\infty$, $\tau_h$, $n_\infty$, $\tau_n$.

Several features are worth noting:

- **Sodium activation ($m$) is extremely fast.** With time constants on the order of 0.1–0.5 ms, the $m$ gate essentially responds instantaneously to voltage changes. This is why some simplified models replace the $m$ ODE with its steady-state approximation: $m \approx m_\infty(V)$.

- **Sodium inactivation ($h$) is slower than activation.** This timing mismatch is essential: the channel must fully open ($m \to 1$) _before_ inactivation kicks in ($h \to 0$). The brief window of time when $m$ is high but $h$ hasn't yet fallen is precisely when the large inward sodium current flows.

- **Potassium activation ($n$) is delayed.** The $n$ gate activates on a similar time scale to $h$, but because it requires all four subunits ($n^4$), the effective rise of the potassium conductance is even more delayed. This delay is critical for repolarization: the potassium current turns on _after_ the sodium current has been inactivated, pulling the voltage back down.

- **Temperature scaling.** All of these rate functions were measured at $6.3°\mathrm{C}$ (the temperature of the seawater where Hodgkin and Huxley's squid lived). To use them at mammalian body temperature ($37°\mathrm{C}$), each rate must be scaled using a $Q_{10}$ factor—a concept we first encountered in the physical chemistry module when studying the Arrhenius equation. Typical $Q_{10}$ values for ion channels are 2–4, meaning the rates roughly triple for every $10°\mathrm{C}$ increase.

## A note on numerical stability

Some of the rate functions above contain expressions of the form $\frac{x}{1 - e^{-x}}$, which become $0/0$ when $x = 0$. Mathematically, this singularity is removable — L'Hôpital's rule gives a finite limit — but a naive code implementation will crash with a division-by-zero error. The standard workaround is to detect when $|x|$ is very small and replace the expression with its Taylor series approximation ($\approx 1 + x/2$). This trick is sometimes called `vtrap` after its implementation in the NEURON simulator, and we will explore it in a later exercise.
