# Modeling molecular transitions with mass action

We are now ready to develop the mathematical framework for ion channel gating. The key idea—and one that connects directly to what we learned in the physical chemistry module—is that the opening and closing of an ion channel is a _chemical reaction_ that can be described using the law of mass action.

## From channels to conductances

Let us start from the Ohmic equation for an ion current passing through a population of channels of type $\mathrm{X}$:

$$
I_{\mathrm{X}} = g_{\mathrm{X}} (V - E_{\mathrm{X}})
$$

The term $g_{\mathrm{X}}$ represents the total conductance of all the channels of this type in a given patch of membrane. As we discussed in the previous chapter, we can express this macroscopic conductance explicitly in terms of the underlying single channels:

$$
g_{\mathrm{X}} = N_{\mathrm{X}} \cdot G_{\mathrm{X}} \cdot P_o
$$

where $N_{\mathrm{X}}$ is the total number of channels, $G_{\mathrm{X}}$ is the conductance of a _single_ open channel, and $P_o$ is the **open probability**—the fraction of channels that are currently open.

Because $N_{\mathrm{X}}$ and $G_{\mathrm{X}}$ are physical constants for a given cell, they are typically combined into a single maximum conductance parameter, denoted with an overbar: $\bar{g}_{\mathrm{X}} = N_{\mathrm{X}} G_{\mathrm{X}}$. This is the conductance that would result if every single channel were open simultaneously. Our current equation becomes:

$$
I_{\mathrm{X}} = \bar{g}_{\mathrm{X}} \cdot P_o \cdot (V - E_{\mathrm{X}})
$$

Everything in this equation is either a fixed parameter ($\bar{g}_{\mathrm{X}}$, $E_{\mathrm{X}}$) or a known variable ($V$). It is only $P_o$ that represents the dynamic, time-varying function of the channel. The core task, therefore, is to find a differential equation that describes how $P_o$ evolves over time.

## Deriving the gating equation

Imagine a large population of identical ion channels sitting in a membrane at a fixed voltage. At any given moment, some fraction of them are open and the rest are closed. Channels are constantly transitioning between these two states: closed channels are opening at some rate, and open channels are closing at some rate.

We can represent this as a reversible reaction between a Closed ($\mathrm{C}$) state and an Open ($\mathrm{O}$) state, with forward rate constant $\alpha$ (opening) and backward rate constant $\beta$ (closing):

$$
\mathrm{C} \underset{\beta}{\overset{\alpha}{\rightleftharpoons}} \mathrm{O}
$$

This should look very familiar from the physical chemistry module! It is precisely the same type of reversible reaction we studied using the law of mass action. Applying mass action, the rate of change of the open-state concentration is:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{O}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = \alpha [\mathrm{C}] - \beta [\mathrm{O}]
$$

Because the total number of channels is conserved ($[\mathrm{C}] + [\mathrm{O}] = \text{const}$), we can divide through by this total and work with _probabilities_ instead of concentrations. If we define $m$ as the probability of being open, then the probability of being closed is $(1 - m)$. Substituting:

```{math}
:label: eq:gating_ode
\frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}t} = \alpha (1 - m) - \beta m
```

This is the fundamental **Hodgkin-Huxley gating equation**. Despite its simplicity, this single ODE captures the essential dynamics of channel gating. The first term ($\alpha(1-m)$) describes closed channels opening; the second term ($\beta m$) describes open channels closing.

## Physical interpretation

This equation has two key properties that are worth understanding intuitively before we proceed to solve it.

**Equilibrium.** If we wait long enough, the system will reach a steady state where $\mathrm{d}m/\mathrm{d}t = 0$. Setting the left-hand side to zero and solving for $m$:

$$
m_\infty = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta}
$$

This is the _steady-state open probability_: the fraction of channels that will be open after the system has had time to fully equilibrate. Notice that $m_\infty$ depends only on the _ratio_ of the rate constants, not their absolute magnitudes.

**Time constant.** The equation can be rewritten in a more revealing form:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{m_\infty - m}{\tau_m}, \qquad \text{where} \quad \tau_m = \frac{1}{\alpha + \beta}
$$

This is a standard **relaxation equation**: it says that $m$ will exponentially approach the target value $m_\infty$ with a time constant $\tau_m$. Large rate constants mean a short time constant (the gate responds quickly). The ratio of the rates determines _where_ the gate settles; the sum of the rates determines _how fast_ it gets there.

## Independent subunits

For some channels, the transition from closed to open is a single, simple step—well described by a single gating variable $m$. However, recall from the previous chapter that tetrameric $\mathrm{K}^+$ channels have _four_ identical voltage sensors, all of which must shift outward before the pore can physically open.

If we assume that each of these four transitions is identical and statistically independent, then the total open probability $P_o$ is simply the product of the individual subunit probabilities:

$$
P_o = n^4
$$

where $n$ represents the probability of a _single_ voltage sensor being in the permissive state, governed by the gating ODE we just derived. When we plug this back into our Ohmic current equation, we get the famous Hodgkin-Huxley formulation for the potassium current:

$$
I_{\mathrm{K}} = \bar{g}_{\mathrm{K}} \, n^4 \, (V - E_{\mathrm{K}})
$$

Similarly, the sodium current in the HH model requires _three_ activation gates ($m$) and one inactivation gate ($h$):

$$
I_{\mathrm{Na}} = \bar{g}_{\mathrm{Na}} \, m^3 \, h \, (V - E_{\mathrm{Na}})
$$

The factor $m^3$ reflects the requirement for three of the four $\mathrm{Na}^+$ channel domains to activate, while $h$ represents the independent inactivation process (such as the ball-and-chain mechanism we discussed earlier).

:::{note}
The gating equation we derived above applies to _any_ gate. We used $m$ as a generic name, but each gate in the HH model — $m$, $h$, and $n$ — obeys its own copy of this same equation, with its own rate constants $\alpha$ and $\beta$.
:::
