# The "zoo" of modern ionic currents

To model complex excitable cells accurately, we must include a wide variety of specialized ion channels. While the exact channel expressions vary dramatically between the brain and the heart, many of the underlying protein families are exactly the same.

Here are the major players in modern electrophysiology models.

## 1. The fast sodium current ($I_{\mathrm{Na}}$)

Just like in the squid axon, voltage-gated sodium channels are responsible for the explosive, rapid upstroke of the action potential in both neurons and cardiac cells. They activate instantly and inactivate almost as quickly.

**The modern twist:** Experimentalists discovered that a tiny fraction of sodium channels fail to inactivate properly, instead rapidly flickering between open and closed states. This creates the **late sodium current ($I_{\mathrm{Na,L}}$)**. While small, this persistent inward current plays a crucial role in maintaining the long plateau of the cardiac action potential, and mutations that increase it can cause lethal arrhythmias.

## 2. The L-type calcium current ($I_{\mathrm{CaL}}$)

This is the defining current of the cardiac action potential. Unlike sodium channels, L-type calcium channels activate and inactivate much more slowly.

When they open, the influx of calcium serves two purposes:

1. It provides a massive inward positive current that balances the outward potassium currents, creating the prolonged **plateau phase** of the cardiac action potential (lasting $\sim$200-300 ms).
2. The incoming calcium physically binds to receptors inside the cell to trigger the mechanical contraction of the heart muscle.

**The modern twist:** $I_{\mathrm{CaL}}$ undergoes _calcium-dependent inactivation_. The channel doesn't just close because of voltage; it physically senses the local intracellular calcium concentration and shuts itself off if the concentration gets too high! This creates a complex mathematical interdependence that is difficult to model with standard Hodgkin-Huxley equations.

## 3. The transient outward potassium current ($I_{\mathrm{to}}$)

In neuroscience, this is famously known as the **A-type potassium current**. It is a rapidly activating, rapidly inactivating outward current.

Because it activates so quickly at the peak of the action potential, it acts as an immediate "brake." In the heart, $I_{\mathrm{to}}$ is responsible for the rapid early repolarization notch immediately following the sodium upstroke. In neurons, varying the density of this current determines whether a neuron fires single spikes or rapid bursts of action potentials.

## 4. The delayed rectifiers ($I_{\mathrm{Kr}}$ and $I_{\mathrm{Ks}}$)

In the classic Hodgkin-Huxley model, there was only one delayed rectifier. Mammalian cells split this job into two distinct currents:

- **$I_{\mathrm{Kr}}$ (Rapid):** Carried by the famous **hERG** channel. This current is vital for the final repolarization of the cell. It has a highly unusual kinetic profile where it inactivates almost instantly upon depolarization, and actually conducts the _most_ current as the cell is repolarizing and recovering from inactivation.
- **$I_{\mathrm{Ks}}$ (Slow):** Activates incredibly slowly over hundreds of milliseconds. It acts as a delayed safety valve, slowly building up strength to ensure the cell eventually repolarizes even if the action potential is abnormally prolonged.

## 5. The inward rectifier ($I_{\mathrm{K1}}$)

This current is the anchor of the cell. It conducts strongly at negative voltages but shuts down completely at depolarized voltages due to intracellular molecules physically plugging the pore.

Its job is to hold the resting membrane potential rock-steady at roughly -80 mV. By shutting down during the action potential, it prevents the cell from "leaking" potassium during the plateau, conserving massive amounts of cellular energy.
